1. Software Processes and the Waterfall Model

Every software project follows some kind of process, whether explicit or implicit. A software process describes how teams are organised to develop a system, which tasks are appointed to people, and the workflow of development activities. The classical reference is the Waterfall model — the (unused) core framework: each stage starts only when the preceding one is completed, possibly with a different team per phase, and with a strong focus on the artifact produced by each stage:

  1. Requirements Engineering — produces the specification: which behaviour fulfils the requirements?
  2. Architectural Design — produces the system architecture: which software macro-structure?
  3. Detailed Design — produces the detailed design document: which software micro-structure?
  4. Implementation — produces the first working code: which runnable code?
  5. Testing & Maintenance — produces correct and tested code: which requirements are met?
  6. Deployment — produces the running system: is the system successfully online?
Key insight

The Waterfall is a predictive method: it requires foreseeing the future. Requirements instability is its primary weakness — anything not foreseen at the start forces going back through the completed stages, which the pure model does not contemplate.

Waterfall Variants

Real projects rarely follow a pure waterfall. Three common adaptations address its rigidity:

VariantIdeaKey characteristic
Waterfall with feedbackEach phase can revise the previous oneGoing back is treated as the norm, not an exception
Overlapping phasesPhases have smooth transitionsDifferent teams keep interacting throughout
Incremental waterfallIsolated increments delivered at different stagesPartial releases provide some features early

Process Model Types Compared

ModelFeatures deliveredAccuracy
Predictive (Waterfall)All at onceFull accuracy from the start
IterativeAll featuresPartial accuracy initially, improved in later versions
IncrementalSome features earlyFull accuracy for delivered features
AgileSome features earlyPartial accuracy initially, improved iteratively
Editor's note

Agile combines iterative and incremental approaches: it delivers working software frequently, each iteration adding features and improving accuracy based on feedback.

The motivation for putting iterative and incremental models together is to build early versions of the system to get feedback, both from stakeholders and internally. Early prototypes can be horizontal (a thin slice of the whole system, e.g. the full UI with no logic) or vertical (one feature developed through all layers). Pros: improving requirements, a common vision, better design. Cons: narrowing the vision, impatience, pressure, expectations, code-orientation.

2. Agile Software Development

The Agile Manifesto (2001, agilemanifesto.org) was signed by many practitioners of the Agile Alliance. It started from criticising the waterfall model and its inability to handle change. The key idea: do not refuse change, rather embrace it.

The 12 Principles of Agile Software

These principles deeply influenced modern software development:

  1. Highest priority: satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.
  2. Welcome changing requirements, even late in development.
  3. Deliver working software frequently (weeks to months, preferring shorter timescales).
  4. Business people and developers must work together daily throughout the project.
  5. Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them.
  6. The most efficient and effective method of conveying information is face-to-face conversation.
  7. Working software is the primary measure of progress.
  8. Agile processes promote sustainable development: constant pace indefinitely.
  9. Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility.
  10. Simplicity — the art of maximising the amount of work not done — is essential.
  11. The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organising teams.
  12. At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts.
The examiner will ask

You should be able to explain the Agile principles and contrast them with the Waterfall approach. Note that Agile is a set of guidelines, not a true process: methodologies such as Scrum instantiate it. The examiner may ask: "Why is 'working software' the primary measure of progress, rather than documentation or design completeness?" Because only running software can be validated by stakeholders and produce the feedback that agility is built on (principles 1–3); documents and designs can look complete while foreseeing the future wrongly, which is exactly the predictive weakness of the Waterfall.

Industry reality

Most industries claim they use "agile methods" at least in part, but typically they just inherit some principles and fear to embrace them all. Scrum is considered the ultimate approach, but needs trust, experience, and tailoring: some industries apparently use a good 80–90% of it.

3. Scrum: Roles and Artifacts

Scrum is an iterative and incremental agile framework for managing product development: "a flexible, holistic product development strategy where a development team works as a unit to reach a common goal". It enables teams to self-organise by encouraging physical co-location, daily face-to-face, or close online collaboration. The name comes from the rugby term "scrum" (Italian: mischia), coined by Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber in 1993, inspired by the 1986 paper "The New Product Development Game" by Takeuchi and Nonaka. The course follows "The Scrum Primer 2.0".

Scrum answers five key questions: how to break a long (months) development into pieces/milestones; how to split a large (≥ 5 developers) development among team members; how to regulate the relationships between team, product manager and stakeholders; how to manage the selection of priorities throughout; how to track the evolution and state of development.

Scrum Roles

RoleGoalKey responsibilities
Product Owner (PO)One person responsible for maximising return on investmentInteracts with customers, identifies features, creates and maintains the priority list (Product Backlog), giving a meaning to "value" (commercial value, customer satisfaction, strategic objectives, attacking risks). A sort of product manager, with a difference: the PO understands product development, continuously interacts with the team and reviews Sprint results, but is not accountable for fine-grained decisions.
Development TeamBuild the product the PO indicatesCross-functional and multi-learning, self-organising, autonomous and accountable, 5–9 people with no internal structure, 100% dedicated to one project during the Sprint, typically stable across projects; members together cover analysis, development, testing, interface and database design, architecture, documentation (big projects use flat "feature teams"). They estimate time, decide which items to implement in a Sprint, and provide ideas to the PO.
Scrum Master (SM)Help the group (PO, team, organisation) learn and apply ScrumA coach, not a leader, manager or representative. Helps smooth coordination between PO and Team; useful when moving towards an agile context, typically not needed by a very experienced team. Not the PO (which would make a micro-managing PO); a person very skilled in Scrum, possibly an external counsellor (there are courses and certifications).

Scrum Artifacts

ArtifactDescription
Product BacklogA sort of product development roadmap: a prioritised list of customer-centric features that exists and evolves over the project lifetime; it is prioritised, emergent and deep (more fine-grained on top). Each item has: priority position, description, possibly a URL to details, estimation size — a relative number of points, so that the team's actual velocity (points per Sprint) emerges. Items include new customer features ("enable GUI-oriented interface"), major engineering improvements ("Java2Scala rewrite of the MVC model"), research work ("investigate approaches to game AI"), improvement goals ("speed up tests"), correcting major known defects, and bootstrap activities ("set up the build environment", "design architecture"); agility requires that most items focus on delivering value to customers.
Sprint BacklogA refinement of the Product Backlog portion for the current Sprint. Each item is broken into Sprint tasks with estimates and person-task assignments (not only by volunteering or skills; typically one task per person at a time; partly dynamic). Often rendered as a board with post-it notes: To-Do, WIP, Done. Changes to the Sprint target necessarily go into the next Sprint; dramatic changes require stopping the current Sprint.
IncrementThe completed Product Backlog items at the end of a Sprint, which must meet the Definition of Done: the "potentially shippable increment".
Definition of Done

When is an item closed? Only when it is definitely "done", according to a definition agreed at the beginning; items not finished are moved to a forthcoming Sprint. Possible facets: software that comes with thorough and non-failing tests, that does not break existing code, that is documented in certain ways, that comes with tutorials, that meets certain quality criteria, that is acceptance-tested. This impacts the notion of "potentially shippable increment".

4. Scrum: Ceremonies and Sprint Lifecycle

A Sprint is a time-boxed iteration (1–4 weeks; the smaller, the more agile). The entire development happens within Sprints, one after the other, each producing a potentially shippable increment; team effectiveness typically increases from Sprint to Sprint. Time-boxes per week of Sprint: Sprint Planning 1 hour per part, Daily Scrum max 15 minutes, Product Backlog Refinement max 10% of the Sprint, Sprint Review 1 hour, Sprint Retrospective 45 minutes.

Part 1 (WHAT): Review high-priority items, discuss goals and context with the PO. Result: Sprint Goal — a summary statement of the Sprint objective.

Part 2 (HOW): Forecast the amount of items completable, starting from the top of the Product Backlog (possibly picking items further down), considering the velocity so far and assuming a member works only 4–6 hours a day. Crucially, decisions are taken by the team, not the PO: the team commits to a forecast, communicated to the PO (who is optional in part 2). The result is the Sprint Backlog. Duration: ~1 hour per part per week of Sprint.

15 minutes daily at the appointed time (team standing). Each member reports: (i) what was done, (ii) what will be done, (iii) what obstacles exist. No in-depth technical discussion — that happens in follow-up meetings. The team updates the Sprint Backlog daily and plots the Sprint burndown chart to track progress.

Inspection and adaptation of the product increment. Whole Scrum Team, possibly stakeholders and users. Often mislabelled "demo", which it is not: it is key for the PO to understand how the product is evolving and for the Team to learn about the PO and the market; it is a PO+Team conversation using live software — no formal presentation with slides. Duration: ~1 hour per week of Sprint.

Inspection and adaptation of the process or environment, not the product. Team, SM, PO optional. Discuss what is working and what is not, and what changes to try (there is a whole catalogue of techniques, "Agile retrospectives"). Focus on both positive and negative aspects. Next Sprint Planning comes immediately after. Duration: ~45 minutes per week of Sprint.

Releases and continuous integration/delivery. It is the PO that decides when and what to actually release; proper technologies must be in place for quick and effective delivery, otherwise a "Release Sprint" is needed. Product Backlog Refinement

Ongoing activity (max 10% of Sprint time) where the team splits big items, analyses, re-estimates, and re-prioritises for future Sprints. Doing this properly makes Sprint Planning quick and effective.

The examiner will ask

What is the Sprint Goal? A summary statement of the Sprint objective, agreed in Sprint Planning part 1, leaving flexibility on the quantity and selection of items. Who decides what goes into a Sprint? The PO decides priorities in the Product Backlog; the Team decides how much it can forecast to complete. What happens if work cannot be completed within the Sprint? Unfinished items go back to the Product Backlog for a forthcoming Sprint; the Sprint is not extended. How does the burndown chart help? Updated daily from the Sprint Backlog estimates, it shows remaining work against time, making early visible whether the forecast will be met.

5. Software Engineering Principles

Software engineering is "concerned with the theories, methods and tools needed to develop software", especially systems so large or complex that they are built by teams. Without a structured methodology one simply starts programming: this cannot address complexity and dynamism, works only for few programmers, and does not handle the intrinsic change of requirements. A development methodology clearly identifies stages and their products, adopts suitable principles and techniques, and sets up a process to allocate and control tasks — the ultimate goal being to make software development predictable. Its ingredients are principles, architecture, specification, verification, process, management and tools; its steps are requirements engineering, architectural design, detailed design, coding, testing, deployment and maintenance; its process can be predictive, adaptive, iterative or agile. Software itself is a multi-artifact asset: code (production, libraries, tests, DevOps, demos), data (input, test, output) and documentation (comments, API docs, tutorials, project and management documentation, issues) — increasingly a "live" artifact.

The principles below form the foundation of good software engineering practice (the acronyms are those of the PPS-01 slides; the methodology deck abbreviates them RF, SOC, M, A, AOC, G, I). You should be able to explain each one and give examples of its application.

AcronymPrincipleMeaningExamples
RFRigour and FormalityDescribe behaviour as precisely as mathematics wouldFinite state machines, DSL-like APIs, rule-like specifications in tests
SOCSeparation of ConcernsMaster complexity by grouping issues, consider each in isolation: separate in time, split qualities (efficiency vs correctness), views (problem vs solution)Split teams, divide tests (unit/integration/acceptance), separate functional from non-functional requirements
MODModularityDivide software in modules with high cohesion and low coupling; top-down or bottom-up; supports reusability and composabilityPackage structure, nested classes, deployment artifacts (JARs, DLLs)
ABSAbstractionIdentify key aspects and ignore unnecessary detailsInterfaces, API layers, DSLs, choosing the right paradigm
AOCAnticipation of ChangeEvolution is inherent to software, unlike other engineering fields: create the ground for flexible reactivity to likely changesAbstract classes, constants instead of magic numbers, encapsulate implementation choices
GENGeneralitySolve a more general problem than the one at hand, promoting reuse and layeringParameterise implementation choices, support more input cases than currently needed
INCIncrementalityA quality of software processes: reason in terms of smaller and smaller incrementsVersion numbering, Scrum sprints, TDD red-green-refactor cycles
Key idea

These principles are well-known and universally agreed upon — yet developers often believe they have "no time" to follow them. The real skill is knowing when to apply each one judiciously.

6. Requirement Specification

Terminology varies (specification, analysis, requirements engineering); the course's term is (software) requirements specification (SRS), and its scope is everything that goes before, and fully grounds, design and implementation. A stakeholder is a person involved in the project (buyer, manager, client, user — not a developer); a requirement is a record of a stakeholder goal or need; a requirement specification is a description of precisely what will be done to achieve a requirement — same shape as a requirement, deeper in detail. The goal of the stage is to turn abstract requirements into well-engineered specifications.

Complete vs partial specification

Two schools: a complete SRS fully characterises system behaviour and qualities; a partial one only tracks and formalises stakeholder needs. The course adheres to completeness: we do not care if stakeholders were incomplete, we analyse and fill all the gaps, because a software system is a system first and its behaviour should be understood before design/implementation. Design is about how the software is organised to meet the specification: it does not decide how the system behaves. Agility only means that specification is ongoing rather than an initial phase: completing it throughout or at the end is fine. Key rule: starting from the same specification, two diverging teams should reach equivalent systems; capturing entirely what a system must do is also what makes good tests possible and lets you validate code produced by third parties, humans or not.

Types of Requirements

TypeQuestion answeredExamples
Business RequirementsWhy is this software strategic?Project goals, ROI expectations, success criteria
Functional RequirementsWhat functionalities does the system provide?User requirements (how users interact) and System requirements (internal behaviour, rules, constraints)
Non-functional RequirementsWhat qualities must the system have?Performance, scalability, reliability, security, usability
Implementation RequirementsWhat constraints exist on production (and why)?Technologies, training, material, internal quality — anticipating details of architecture/design/process, but they must not anticipate design decisions

Requirements are recorded with user stories (a short prose story of how the system lets the user do things), use cases (a series of interactions between actors), prototypes (software mock-ups), UML class diagrams (domain modelling) and interaction diagrams (sequences of actions), formulas, pseudo-code and tables.

Common pitfalls

A requirement must be validatable, by definition: if it is so informal that it cannot be objectively validated, it is not a requirement, it is a principle. Bad examples: "the user can insert a product" (which product data?), "the system should be scalable/fast" (how fast? scalable with what?), "the code should be modular" (a principle, not a requirement), "should use JavaFX" (is it not a motivated design choice?), "the system should have no bugs" (trivial). Requirements should be numbered and structured, so that design and implementation can mention them throughout, and it should be tracked how each was validated: user requirements by acceptance tests, system requirements by automated tests (e.g. references to JUnit tests), non-functional requirements by stress tests or acceptance tests.

Requirements Engineering Process

  1. Feasibility study — estimate whether user needs can be satisfied cost-effectively.
  2. Requirements analysis — derive abstract requirements from existing systems, user discussion, task analysis.
  3. Requirements definition — translate analysis into a structured set of requirements (functional, non-functional, etc.), understandable by the customers.
  4. Requirements specification — make requirements detailed and precise, used as the basis for a contract and for development coherence/compliance.
  5. Requirements maintenance — retrofitting new choices/features as they emerge during development, completed a posteriori to formalise the result.

The Domain Model

Software developers must become experts in the domain at hand (covid infection, chess rules, agriculture, ships and aliens), and the team must reach a common understanding of it. A domain model is a formal description of the key aspects of the domain that the software is about. It should be complete and cover all useful information about terminology, structure, and behaviour. UML diagrams (class diagram with good names and few methods, object, sequence, activity) with prose descriptions are the typical formalism — not necessarily matching the design.

The examiner will ask

You should understand the Pacman example: the requirement "there are 4 types of enemies, as in the standard videogame" becomes a specification with the details — Blinky (red) targets the player's current location; Pinky (pink) targets the position four spaces ahead of the player's direction of travel; Inky (cyan) uses the player's forward direction as a reference point, then offsets that target based on Blinky's position; Clyde (orange) pursues the player while at a distance, and once near switches to his own scatter area (usually a lower corner). Why: we have to clarify what we expect those enemies to do, in a way that makes the result objectively "successful" or "not".

7. Design: Architectural and Detailed

Design turns the requirement specification into a detailed implementation schema used by developers: study the requirements, identify one solution, describe the abstractions needed for it, iteratively. It is a creative process based on experience, with no true cook-book beyond existing patterns and principles. A design can be seen as a graph of entities (types, components) with relations (uses, inherits, calls), formalised and refined iteratively along many dimensions — architecture, abstract specification, interfaces, components, data structures, algorithms — conceptually in this order, practically not. There is no design without a good description of it: design is software organisation motivated by principles and requirements, implementation is code that just works; if it is not described in the documentation, anyone will assume the code organisation "just happened" without understanding its implications.

Architectural Design

Defines the strategic aspects: frameworks, tools, paradigms, high-level principles, main sub-systems and their boundaries, contracts, and roles.

Detailed Design

Defines the tactical aspects: class-level decisions, interfaces, data structures, algorithms. Can rely on design patterns (GoF) and programming idioms.

Editor's note

Architecture and architectural patterns are distinct: an architecture is a division into named components with defined roles and interactions; an architectural pattern is a reusable template. The same relationship holds between "design" and "design patterns".

Design Recipes

StageKey recipes
Requirement specification Visionary (business requirements clarify what a successful project is: the high-level acceptance criteria); verifiable (clearly stated and factual); result-complete (every system feature is traceable to a requirement, otherwise it is an arbitrary choice); agreement-complete (detailed enough to define a contract with the customer); iterative (it is software: it evolves with code); self-contained (the document is standalone); prescriptive (different teams would develop it into equivalent systems)
Architectural design Technology-deferring (described independently of technologies); abstract but complete (abstract boundaries, but roles and interactions clearly stated); requirement-enabling (choices needed to guarantee requirements are taken here); iterative (though it typically does not evolve much); self-contained (requirements + architecture are standalone)
Detailed design Technology-dependent (technology constrains the developer, so it is decided here); complete (a choice not written here is not a choice: it "happened"); formal (UML diagrams); still abstract (not just the diagram of an implementation); iterative (evolves frequently with code); self-contained (requirements + architectural + detailed design are standalone)

The other stages: development is the realm of good coding techniques (covered throughout the course); testing goes from unit testing up to acceptance testing; deployment and maintenance are modernly framed as continuous integration/delivery.

8. UML Diagrams

Requirements become design, which becomes code: eventually design becomes formal, expressing computational structure and behaviour non-ambiguously, through representational/programming languages or visual diagrams. Unified Modelling Language (UML) diagrams are the standard visual formalism: not boring bureaucracy, but powerful design tools, especially to address complexity, and using a standard gives value. UML structural diagrams: class, component, object, package; behavioural diagrams: state machine, activity, sequence, use case. For concurrency and distribution there are also nets (automata, Petri nets, workflow diagrams), process algebras, and any model-to-model representation turned into code.

9. Documentation and the Exam Project

Documentation is an essential part of quality software. Key facts to remember:

  • Documentation is software: handle it with analogous care, applying DRY and KISS.
  • It grows and refines alongside code — neither purely preliminary nor purely final.
  • It reifies principles, guidelines, and choices not clearly evident in the code.
  • Project documentation documents the transition from requirements (what) to a compliant implementation (how): it is a reference for the team, for manager vs team and vs stakeholders, and for future changes; it tracks goals, choices and motivations; it mediates the inescapable conflicts; and it is what lets you find why a problem happened and where to act. Investing time in it brings a notable return.
  • For the exam project, the documentation ends up in the project report. The report must help the teacher evaluate the project, answers to questions about the project should already be in the report, and key decisions are to be discussed and motivated.
Exam project process

The suggested process for the course project adapts Scrum: one student (e.g. who had the project idea) acts as client/domain expert as well as developer, ensuring usability and quality of the result; another (with coordination skills) acts as Product Owner as well as developer. In an initial meeting the team prepares a Product Backlog and defines a first organisational Sprint covering the build setup, basic requirements and architecture, and the elements of the process (Sprint duration, Definition of Done, types and frequency of meetings). Sprints should be short (~15 hours of work over one week, so that around four Sprints are completed), each yielding tangible results of value for the stakeholders (the teacher or the student/client). The team maintains a Sprint Backlog, holds frequent meetings plus meetings at the beginning and end of each Sprint, and produces a very brief report at each Sprint's end, kept under version control.

Check Your Understanding

Explain the difference between predictive, iterative, incremental, and agile process models. Which one does Scrum belong to?

Predictive (Waterfall) delivers all features at once with full accuracy planned upfront. Iterative delivers all features but with partial accuracy, improving over time. Incremental delivers some features early with full accuracy. Agile combines iterative and incremental: delivers some features early with partial accuracy, then adds features and improves accuracy. Scrum is an agile framework.

What are the three Scrum roles, and what is the primary responsibility of each?

Product Owner: maximises ROI, maintains the Product Backlog, interacts with customers. Development Team (5-9 people): self-organising, cross-functional, builds the product, estimates and decides Sprint items. Scrum Master: coach who helps the group learn and apply Scrum, smooths coordination, not a manager or leader.

What is the Definition of Done, and why is it important?

It is an agreed-upon set of criteria that must be met for an item to be considered "done". Facets include: thorough and passing tests, no breakage of existing code, documentation, quality criteria, acceptance testing. It impacts the notion of "potentially shippable increment" and ensures consistent quality across the team.

What does "Rigour and Formality" mean in software engineering? Give an example.

It means describing or enacting behaviour as precisely as mathematics would. It is key where errors would incur high technical debt. Examples: using finite state machines, Petri nets, DSL-like APIs for configuration, rule-like specifications in tests.

Why must a requirement be verifiable? Give an example of a bad (non-verifiable) requirement and how to fix it.

A requirement that cannot be objectively validated is not a true requirement — it is just a principle. Bad: "the system should be fast". Fixed: "the system should respond to user queries within 200ms under a load of 1000 concurrent users".

Explain the difference between architectural and detailed design. Give an example of each.

Architectural design defines strategic aspects: which architectural pattern (MVC, Layers), which frameworks, tools and paradigms, the main sub-systems with their boundaries, contracts and roles. Detailed design defines tactical aspects: how code is formally organised in classes and interfaces, using design patterns (GoF), idioms and refactorings. Example: choosing MVC is architectural; deciding to use the Observer pattern within the Model is detailed design. The distinction is blurred but useful; note that the architectural recipe is technology-deferring, while the detailed design is technology-dependent.

Should a requirement specification be complete or partial? What is the "key rule" used to judge it?

The course adheres to the school of completeness: the SRS should fully characterise the behaviour and qualities of the system, filling the gaps left by stakeholders, because the system's behaviour must be understood before design, which only decides how the software is organised to meet the specification. The key rule is prescriptiveness: starting from the same specification, two diverging design/development teams should reach equivalent systems. Completeness is also what makes good tests identifiable and lets you validate code produced by third parties. In an agile setting specification is ongoing, so it may be completed throughout or at the end.

Who serves as Scrum Master, and why should it not be the Product Owner?

The Scrum Master is a coach helping PO, team and organisation learn and apply Scrum: not a leader, manager or representative; a person very skilled in Scrum, possibly an external counsellor, often unnecessary for very experienced teams. It must not be the PO, because a PO acting as SM would become a micro-managing one, conflicting with the principle that the team self-organises and decides how much to forecast and how to implement it.

What is the velocity of a team, and how is it used in Sprint Planning?

Backlog items are estimated with relative sizes (points); the velocity is the number of points a team actually completes per Sprint, which emerges from past Sprints. In Sprint Planning part 2 the team uses it (together with the assumption that a member works 4–6 productive hours a day) to forecast how many items from the top of the Product Backlog can be completed; the forecast is the team's commitment, communicated to the PO, and the Sprint burndown chart tracks daily whether it is being met.
