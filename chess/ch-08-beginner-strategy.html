1. Tactics versus strategy

Tactics, from Chapter Seven, are short forcing sequences that win material or deliver mate right away. Strategy is the longer view: reading a position where material is level and deciding who stands better and why, then building a plan — pressuring the enemy kingside, say, or grinding toward a favorable endgame — that doesn't pay off immediately but improves your chances over many moves. This chapter covers three of the biggest strategic ideas for a beginner: weaknesses, space, and the difference between closed and open positions.

2. Weaknesses

A weakness is a piece, pawn, or square that isn't adequately defended and can be exploited, either right away or over time. Spotting weaknesses is where strategy and tactics meet: strategic thinking finds the target, and a tactic (often a pin or a fork, from the last chapter) cashes it in.

Start by scanning for undefended pieces — remember, pawns only defend diagonally, so a piece surrounded by pawns isn't necessarily protected by them.

Sometimes a single move creates two weaknesses to attack at once — this is exactly the fork idea from Chapter Seven, found through strategic scanning rather than a tactic that was already sitting on the board.

Weaknesses don't have to be dramatic. Even a single extra pawn in an endgame, won by attacking a target that has no way to gain a second defender, is a meaningful and often decisive strategic gain.

Squares can be weak too, not just pieces. A square is weak when it's poorly defended and, crucially, when the nearby pawns are mostly on the opposite color — since a pawn only ever defends squares that match the color it stands on, a king surrounded by pawns of one color has holes of the other color right next to it.

3. Space

Space means the number of squares you control in the opponent's half of the board — where "control" means one of your pieces could capture anything that landed there. A big space advantage cramps the opponent's pieces and gives yours room to maneuver.

Space isn't permanent — it shifts with every move and can be fought over or traded away. If you're cramped, look to trade off the enemy pieces or pawns that are doing the cramping; the same rook is worth the same three points whether or not it controls half the board, but a rook with room to work is worth far more in practice. If you're the one with more space, be careful about trading pieces just because the trade looks even — you can hand back your entire advantage by simplifying into a position where your extra space no longer matters.

4. Closed versus open positions

Whether a position is open or closed comes down to one thing: where the pawns are. Think of each side's eight pawns as a fence. When the fences are still mostly intact and pressed against each other, the position is closed — pieces have trouble crossing the center, and progress comes from slow maneuvering and pawn breaks rather than direct piece play. The French Defense's Advance Variation is a good illustration of how a closed structure forms and how it's challenged.

The King's Indian Defense is the classic full-board version of this idea: both sides leave the center pawns locked together and race pawn advances on opposite wings, White on the queenside and Black on the kingside, with whoever breaks through first usually winning. The more pawns remain on the board, the more closed — and typically slower and more maneuvering-based — the position tends to be.

An open position is the opposite: pawns have been traded off, and pieces can see and capture across large stretches of the board.

Neither type of position is inherently better — the skill is recognizing which one you're in and playing it appropriately. If your opponent has the bishop pair and you don't, you generally want to keep things closed; if you have it and they don't, you want to open the position up so your bishops can work. In a closed position, look for pawn breaks and piece trades that undermine the opponent's pawn chain rather than trying to force your way through with pieces alone.

5. Summary

Strategy is about reading a position and building a plan over many moves, rather than cashing in an immediate tactic. Weaknesses — undefended pieces, or squares that are poorly guarded and match the "wrong" color of the nearby pawns — are targets worth hunting for constantly, since exploiting one often only needs a tactic you already know. Space, the number of squares you control in enemy territory, cramps the opponent and should be defended by avoiding unnecessary trades when you have it, and fought for by trading away the pieces that are doing the cramping when you don't. Finally, recognize whether a position is closed (pawns locked, slow maneuvering, look for breaks) or open (pawns traded, long diagonals and files, favors bishops and precise calculation), and adjust your plans — and your bishops — accordingly.

Check yourself

How does strategy differ from tactics?

Tactics are short forcing sequences that win material or deliver mate immediately. Strategy is a longer-term plan built from reading who stands better in a position, even when material is level and nothing is immediately forcing.

Why doesn't a pawn always defend the pieces standing next to it?

Pawns only capture and defend diagonally, one square forward. A piece directly in front of, behind, or beside a pawn isn't defended by it at all.

What two conditions make a square "weak" near a king?

It's poorly defended by pieces, and most of the nearby pawns are on the opposite color — since pawns only guard squares matching their own color, that leaves holes of the other color that pieces can walk straight into.

In Board 3, why does Black lose a pawn no matter what they do?

Na5 attacks b7 directly. If Black pushes the pawn to b6 to save it, that same push removes the defender of c6, so the knight simply captures c6 instead.

What does "space" mean in chess, and what should you do if you're short on it?

Space is the number of squares you control in the opponent's half of the board. If you're cramped, look to trade off the specific enemy pieces or pawns that are restricting you — that shifts the balance in your favor.

You have a big space advantage. Why might you want to avoid simplifying trades?

Trading pieces one-for-one is materially even, but it can hand back your positional advantage — a piece is worth more when it controls a lot of space, and fewer pieces on the board means less for your space advantage to constrain.

What single factor determines whether a position is open or closed?

Where the pawns are. Intact, mutually blocking pawn chains make a position closed; pawns that have been traded away open up the files and diagonals.

If your opponent has the bishop pair and you don't, should you aim to keep the position closed or open it up? Why?

Keep it closed. Bishops need open diagonals to be effective, so locking the pawn structure limits exactly the advantage your opponent is holding.
