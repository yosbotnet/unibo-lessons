1. Covering the two big replies

Half of your games will be played with the Black pieces. Since 1.e4 is roughly 2.5 times as common as 1.d4, and the two together make up 90–95 percent of what you will face, a Black repertoire that answers both covers nearly every game. This chapter gives two recommendations against 1.e4, one against 1.d4, and two setup-based defenses that can be used against almost anything. As with White openings, pick one plan per first move and stick to it — that repetition is what turns a memorized reply into real understanding.

Playing Black is inherently more demanding to prepare for than playing White, because White always moves first and Black has to be ready to respond to whatever comes. The upside covered in this chapter is that a handful of well-chosen replies, or a single flexible setup, closes that gap almost entirely — you do not need a separate answer to every White idea you might ever meet, only to the common ones.

2. The Scandinavian Defense

Against 1.e4, the first recommendation is 1...d5 — the Scandinavian Defense. It immediately forces White to make a decision, flipping the usual dynamic where White dictates and Black reacts. White will typically capture, and Black recaptures with the queen:

Watch out

The main danger in the Scandinavian is losing track of the queen on an unsafe square. Remember the sequence: recapture with the queen, retreat to a5 when attacked, and play ...c6 to keep an escape route open.

3. The Caro-Kann Defense

The second recommendation against 1.e4, and the stronger one at higher levels, is the Caro-Kann Defense: 1...c6, preparing ...d5 with the pawn already protected — a "building-block" structure, unlike the Scandinavian's immediate central strike. White then has three main tries: pushing the e-pawn (Advance), capturing on d5 (Exchange), or defending the e-pawn.

Note

The Caro-Kann has been used successfully from beginner to World Champion level. It rewards patient, solid play rather than sharp tactics — a different temperament from the Scandinavian's early complications.

4. The Queen's Gambit Declined

Against 1.d4 — which, unlike 1.e4, cannot be met with the Scandinavian or Caro-Kann — the recommendation is the Queen's Gambit Declined setup. Black mirrors White with 1...d5; if White plays 2.c4 (the Queen's Gambit), Black declines the free pawn and instead plays 2...e6, keeping a solid foothold in the center. That overlap is the whole point: one plan against 1.d4 quietly covers several other White first moves as well, which is exactly the kind of study-time saving worth building into a Black repertoire.

5. Setup-based openings: King's Indian/Pirc and the Dutch

For players who would rather not maintain separate preparation for every White opening, two setups can be played almost universally. Both trade some initiative for consistency: because you cannot fight for central space and active piece play at the same time, you spend more of the game reacting rather than attacking — like a counterpuncher waiting for an opening rather than pressing forward.

Key idea

Setup-based openings save study time and produce consistent, familiar positions early on — but they suit a patient, reactive style. If you enjoy fighting for the initiative from move one, a dedicated response to each of White's main tries will usually suit you better.

6. Chapter summary

Against 1.e4, the Scandinavian and the Caro-Kann are the two recommended defenses — the Scandinavian riskier because of the early queen sortie, the Caro-Kann slower but very solid. Against 1.d4 (and by extension the English and the Réti), the Queen's Gambit Declined setup offers principled central play, with the c- and d-pawns working together and the light-squared bishop rerouted via b6/b7 once it is blocked in. For a lower-maintenance repertoire, the King's Indian/Pirc and the Dutch Defense are universal setups that trade some initiative for consistency and less preparation. Whichever combination you choose, commit to it, build your experience through repetition, and use games you lose as material for review rather than reasons to switch openings again.

Check yourself

Why does covering just 1.e4 and 1.d4 prepare you for the vast majority of games with Black?

1.e4 is about 2.5 times as common as 1.d4, and together the two make up roughly 90–95 percent of all games, so a solid answer to each covers nearly everything you will actually face.

What is the main idea behind the Scandinavian Defense (1...d5), and what is its main risk?

It forces White to make an immediate decision, putting White on the back foot rather than Black. The risk is bringing the queen out early: it must be moved to safety (typically to a5) and given an escape route (...c6) or it can come under repeated attack.

How does the Caro-Kann Defense (1...c6) differ structurally from the Scandinavian?

The Caro-Kann prepares ...d5 with the pawn already protected by the c-pawn — a "building-block" approach — rather than striking in the center immediately and recapturing with the queen as the Scandinavian does.

What are White's three main options against the Caro-Kann, and how does Black meet the Advance Variation?

White can push the e-pawn (Advance), capture on d5 (Exchange), or defend the e-pawn. Against the Advance (3.e5), Black strikes back with 3...c5, sacrificing the c-pawn to undermine White's center.

In the Queen's Gambit Declined setup, why does Black often play ...b6 and ...Bb7 rather than developing the light-squared bishop naturally?

The pawn on e6 blocks the bishop's normal diagonals (f5 and g4), so Black reroutes it to b7 via a fianchetto instead, where it supports the d5 pawn and points along a useful diagonal.

What is a "setup-based" opening, and what do you give up by playing one?

It is a fixed formation of pawns and pieces (like the King's Indian/Pirc or the Dutch) that can be reached against almost any opposing setup, saving preparation time. The tradeoff is reduced initiative — you tend to react to your opponent's plans rather than fight actively for space.

Why can the Dutch Defense (1...f5) not be played against 1.e4?

White can simply capture the f5 pawn for free, since nothing defends it after 1.e4 f5. The Dutch only works against openings other than 1.e4.

What is the key structural difference between the Leningrad Dutch and the other Dutch setup described in this chapter?

The Leningrad fianchettoes the dark-squared bishop to g7, King's Indian style, alongside the advanced f-pawn. The other setup keeps the bishop on e7 instead and completes the center with ...d5, trading some dynamism for extra king safety.
