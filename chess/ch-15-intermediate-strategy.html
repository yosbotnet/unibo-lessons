1. Pawns: the fence and its cracks

Pawns are worth only one point each, but collectively they're worth eight — more than any single piece but the queen — and they shape everything else on the board. The overall arrangement of pawns in a position is called its pawn structure, and most openings steer toward a handful of recognizable structures. Learning the structure typical of your openings tells you where the plans are before you've even found them over the board.

A pawn break is a forward pawn push aimed at provoking a favorable trade — usually one that opens a file or diagonal for your pieces, or clears a square for a piece to land on.

Pawns also define pawn complexes — which color squares your pawns occupy. A structure heavy on one color (as in the Maróczy Bind, with several White pawns on light squares) makes that side strong on those squares but porous on the other color, since there are fewer pawns left to challenge pieces landing there. This is also why trading away the bishop that matches your weak color complex is often a good idea, while trading the bishop that reinforces your strong complex gives up needed cover.

Similar logic explains why the King's Indian Defense often splits into races on opposite wings — Black pushes kingside pawns where Black has more space, White pushes queenside pawns where White has more space — and why the Caro-Kann Advance revolves around Black's ...c5 and ...f6 breaks chipping at White's spatial center.

Watch out

Pawn storms — marching a cluster of pawns at the enemy king — only work when you're pushing pawns that aren't sheltering your own king. That's why storms are most common (and safest) when the players have castled on opposite sides.

Beyond the opening, several pawn weaknesses and strengths recur constantly:

  • Pawn islands / majorities — groups of pawns on connected files. Having more pawns than your opponent on one wing (a majority) is an asset for creating a passed pawn in the endgame.
  • Isolated pawn — no friendly pawn on either neighboring file, so it can never be defended by another pawn.
  • Backward pawn — has neighbors, but can't safely advance because the square ahead of it is controlled by the opponent, and no pawn defends it from behind.
  • Doubled pawns — two pawns of the same color on one file, usually from a capture.
  • Pawn chain — a diagonal line of pawns, each defended by the one behind it (except the pawn at the base).

Doubled pawns are genuinely controversial — they're less mobile and the front pawn isn't defended by the one behind it, but they aren't automatically bad. They can add extra defensive coverage and, since the trade that created them usually clears a file or diagonal, they can free up a bishop or queen that was blocked before.

2. Bishops and knights

Both minor pieces are worth three points on paper, but plenty of strong players — Bobby Fischer and Garry Kasparov among them — rated the bishop slightly higher, around 3.15–3.25 to the knight's 3.0. The reason is reach: a bishop on a central square of an empty board covers 13 squares; a knight on the same square covers only 8.

A bishop can also fully dominate a knight in a way that never happens in reverse — a knight can never trap a bishop on an empty board.

Bishops shine in open positions and can influence the game from far away — a bishop on the long diagonal can control squares clear across the board. Knights are the opposite: reliable in any kind of position, able to hop over pieces and pawns that would block a bishop entirely, but needing to get physically close to have any effect. That's exactly what makes an outpost — a square, usually in enemy territory, that no enemy pawn can ever attack and that isn't worth trading a lesser piece to remove — so valuable for a knight in particular: unlike a bishop, a knight defended by a pawn can still see every square it attacks, since the defending pawn doesn't block its own lines of sight the way it would a bishop's diagonal.

Knights can also team up on a single square in a way bishops never can, since the two bishops run on opposite colors forever. Having both bishops while your opponent has lost one is generally an edge at higher levels — unless the opponent locks their remaining pawns onto the color that bishop doesn't use.

On balance, a bishop is the slightly stronger piece, especially as the board opens up — which is why giving one up for a knight without a concrete reason is a habit worth avoiding.

3. Rooks

Rooks start tucked in the corners and, worth five points, are the strongest piece before the queen — a rook covers 14 squares whether it sits in the center or the corner of an empty board, which is part of why it outclasses a bishop or knight (both see far fewer squares from a corner than from the center). A rook is also the only minor-to-major piece, besides the queen, that can force checkmate together with a lone king.

Rooks tend to arrive late. After the other pieces are out and the king is safe, rooks want open files — files with few or no pawns — since an open file lets a rook pressure the whole length of the board.

Rooks can also double up horizontally rather than on a single file — most famously by both landing on the seventh (or second) rank, where they attack every pawn still on that rank and can hunt the enemy king with a series of checks. Two rooks working together can force mate even out in the open, with no other pieces helping.

A rook's relative value also shifts with the position — sometimes it's worth sacrificing the exchange (a rook for a bishop or knight) for compensation, whether that's an immediate attack on the king or long-term damage to the opponent's structure. A rook lift — swinging a rook up and across rather than just sliding it to a central file — is a common way to bring it into a kingside attack when the center or open files aren't available.

4. The queen

Bringing the queen out early is risky, as Chapter One's Scholar's Mate discussion covered — a lone queen is up against 39 points of enemy material, and without support she can't do much before getting chased around and losing time. The queen's real strength shows once she coordinates with other pieces.

A queen and rook lined up on the same file, rank, or diagonal form a powerful battery, as do a queen and bishop. The rarer, and arguably most dangerous, combination is queen and knight: because the knight attacks in an L-shape no other piece can copy, the two together can cover squares that would otherwise need three separate pieces to control.

A lone queen is still exceptionally dangerous on its own, especially for forks that combine a check with an attack on a second, undefended piece.

No other piece can reach a diagonal square from a straight line, or vice versa, in a single move the way a queen can — which is exactly what makes queen forks so hard to see coming and so hard to escape cleanly.

Note

The queen is your second most valuable piece after the king, so get in the habit of checking its safety as often as you check your king's. Trading queens is neither always good nor always bad — it depends entirely on whether the resulting position favors you, particularly whether you're up or down material once the queens are gone.

5. Trading pieces

Early on, it's tempting to judge every trade purely by the point count — a bishop for a knight, a rook for a rook, all "even." But a trade changes the whole position, not just the material count: pieces suddenly see squares they didn't before, files open, and one side's plans get easier while the other's get harder. Evaluating a trade means looking at all of that, not just the arithmetic.

Opening trades are their own category — some captures are simply necessary (declining them lets the opponent dominate the center), and some voluntary trades throw away your only developed piece for nothing. The general habit worth building is keeping the tension: leaving pieces or pawns that could capture each other alone, and developing around them, rather than resolving every tension the moment it appears.

Rule of thumb

If you can't explain out loud what a trade actually gains you, don't make it. Equal material after a trade doesn't mean the resulting position is equal.

Two traps are worth calling out specifically. First, giving up a good piece for a bad one is never justified by the point count alone — trading an actively posted knight for a bishop that's still asleep on its home square loses far more than the numbers suggest. Second, watch for trades that look materially even but aren't: giving up a bishop and knight (six points) for a rook and pawn (also six) usually leaves you structurally worse, since two flexible minor pieces are typically worth more in practice than a rook that hasn't found a job yet, especially early in the game.

6. The full checklist

The CCA checklist from Chapter Fourteen — checks, captures, attacks — gets one more layer at this level: before running it for yourself, ask "what does my opponent want?" Sometimes the threat is obvious; sometimes it takes real effort to see. Skipping this question is how good players still walk into bad positions — you can play a perfectly reasonable-looking move and still miss that it ignores your opponent's actual plan.

Putting it together: scan for your opponent's threat first, then run checks, captures, and attacks for both sides, evaluating rather than just listing them. If none of those apply, look for a move that simply improves your position — more active piece placement, more space, a better pawn structure.

Learning from mistakes

Even strong players skip steps under pressure. Castling into a pawn storm because you only checked your own king's safety, grabbing a pawn without asking why your opponent just "blundered" a piece next to it, or attacking a pawn with a rook without checking whether the rook itself could get trapped — all of these come from running only half the checklist. The fix is always the same: pause and ask what the opponent is threatening before deciding your move is safe.

7. Where to go next

This chapter closes out the intermediate material in this course, but it's nowhere near the end of what there is to learn — chess doesn't have a ceiling. From here, progress mostly comes from independent study rather than another chapter: solving large numbers of tactics puzzles until patterns become instant recognition, reviewing your own games (ideally with an engine) to find out exactly where you went wrong and why, and studying openings in more depth once you have a feel for the resulting structures and plans.

A few habits are worth carrying forward specifically: keep running the CCA checklist — for both sides — on every move, not just when something looks sharp. Keep asking what your opponent wants before deciding your own plan is safe. And keep a mental glossary of the terms this course has introduced — opposition, zugzwang, outposts, batteries, zwischenzugs, and the rest — since naming a pattern is often the first step to recognizing it instantly the next time it appears.

Above all, keep playing and keep reviewing. Ratings climb through repetition, not through reading alone — every blundered queen and missed tactic is part of the same process that eventually makes those mistakes rare.

8. Chapter summary

Pawns form the skeleton of every position: their structure, breaks, complexes, chains, and weaknesses (isolated, backward, doubled) shape what every other piece can do. Bishops edge out knights as the generally stronger minor piece thanks to their reach, though knights hold their own in closed positions and on outposts, where a defending pawn doesn't block their own attack the way it would a bishop's diagonal. Rooks arrive late but hit hardest on open files and the seventh rank, especially working in pairs. The queen is devastating once supported — batteries with a rook or bishop, and especially with a knight — but a liability when overextended alone or left defending passively.

Trading is a skill of its own: material counts matter far less than what a trade does to the resulting position, and the "do trade / don't trade" guidelines in this chapter are starting points, not laws. Finally, the CCA checklist, extended with "what does my opponent want?", is the closest thing this course offers to a universal move-selection habit — not a substitute for calculation, but a discipline that keeps you from missing what's already on the board.

Check yourself

What is a pawn break, and why would you play one even though it can look like it weakens your structure?

A pawn break is a forward push aimed at provoking a trade that benefits you — typically by opening a file or diagonal for your pieces or clearing a square for a piece to land on. It's worth it even at some structural cost because the resulting activity or space usually outweighs the loosened pawns.

What's the difference between an isolated pawn and a backward pawn?

An isolated pawn has no pawn of its own color on either neighboring file, so it can never be defended by a pawn. A backward pawn does have neighbors, but it can't safely advance because the opponent controls the square ahead of it, and no pawn behind it can support the push.

Are doubled pawns always a weakness? Why or why not?

No. They're less mobile and the front pawn isn't defended by the one behind it, but the capture that creates them often opens a file or diagonal for a rook, queen, or bishop, and they can add useful defensive coverage. They're only clearly bad when they're also isolated or backward, with no compensation for the lost mobility.

Why is a bishop generally considered slightly stronger than a knight?

Reach: a bishop on a central square of an empty board covers 13 squares versus a knight's 8, and a bishop can shift from one side of the board to the other in a single move, which a knight needs several moves to do. A knight compensates by working in any kind of position, hopping over blockades, and being especially strong on outposts, where a defending pawn doesn't block its own line of attack.

What makes a square a good outpost for a knight?

It's usually in the opponent's territory, no enemy pawn can ever attack it, and removing the piece there would cost the opponent more material than it's worth (a rook or queen instead of a minor piece, say). Outposts favor knights over bishops because a pawn defending a knight doesn't block the knight's own attack the way a defending pawn blocks a bishop's diagonal.

Why do rooks want open files, and what's special about doubling them on the seventh rank?

An open file has no pawns blocking the rook's path, so it can pressure the entire length of the board. Two rooks doubled on the seventh (or second) rank attack every remaining pawn on that rank simultaneously and can often deliver a forced mating sequence of checks against a king still on the back rank.

Why is queen and knight considered such a dangerous combination, more than queen and rook or queen and bishop?

A knight attacks in an L-shape that no other piece can replicate, so a queen and knight together can cover combinations of squares that would otherwise take three pieces to control. Unlike a queen-rook or queen-bishop battery, they never line up on the same file, rank, or diagonal — they always attack from different angles at once.

What's wrong with trading a bishop and knight for a rook and pawn, even though both sides add up to six points?

The point count is equal, but the pieces aren't: two flexible minor pieces are usually worth more in practice than a rook that hasn't yet found an open file to use, especially early in the game when the rook is undeveloped. The trade tends to leave the side giving up the minor pieces effectively down a piece in everything but name.

What question does this chapter add to the CCA checklist, and why does it matter?

"What does my opponent want?" It matters because checks, captures, and attacks only cover your own forcing options — without first identifying your opponent's plan or threat, you can play a reasonable-looking move that completely ignores what they're about to do, which is how strong players still occasionally blunder into lost positions.
