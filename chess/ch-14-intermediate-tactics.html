1. Tactics are pattern recognition

Chapter Seven introduced tactics as one- or two-move opportunities to win material or force checkmate. As an intermediate player, the goal shifts: tactics stop being something you calculate from scratch every time and start being something you recognize. Strong players aren't smarter in the moment — they've simply seen thousands of similar positions before, so a winning shape jumps out instantly instead of requiring fresh analysis. That recognition is a trained skill, built by solving many puzzles, not something you're born with.

Note

The only real way to build this pattern library is repetition: solving tactics puzzles by the hundreds, ideally the thousands, so your brain starts flagging familiar shapes automatically.

2. Checks, Captures, Attacks (CCA)

The single most useful habit for finding tactics — and for playing sound chess generally — is the CCA checklist: on every move, scan for checks, captures, and attacks, for both yourself and your opponent. These are the forcing moves. A forcing move narrows your opponent's replies from dozens down to one or two, which is exactly what makes a position calculable. A quiet move might allow thirty replies you can't hope to work through; a check might allow only one.

When several checks are available, don't just play the flashiest one — evaluate the check that leaves your opponent the fewest replies first, since it's usually the easiest to calculate to the end. Here's a composed position that rewards exactly this kind of scan: White has several ways to give check, but only one leads all the way to mate.

CCA also flags moves that look automatic but aren't the best choice. If one of your pieces comes under attack, your first instinct might be to save it — but check the CCA list before you do. Sometimes a check elsewhere is worth more than the piece under threat, an idea worth calling danger levels: ignore a small threat if you have a bigger one of your own.

3. Forcing a mate in three

The same checklist scales up to longer forced sequences. When your opponent is one move from mating you, every one of your own moves has to be a check (or otherwise deal with the threat) until you either escape or turn the tables. Below, White is under exactly that pressure and has to keep finding only-moves.

Rule of thumb

"Mate in three" means there's a sequence where, no matter what the opponent does, mate follows in exactly three moves. Verify each of your opponent's replies really is forced — if they have a second legal option you haven't checked, your "forced mate" might not be forced at all.

4. CCA when nothing mates outright

Most positions won't end in immediate checkmate — CCA is just as useful for finding the move that wins material and leaves you winning later. Run the checklist in order: look for checks first (and reject the ones that don't work), then captures, then attacks.

None of this wins the game outright, but it wins a full piece for nothing — a decisive material edge to nurse into the endgame. This is exactly why CCA matters beyond mating attacks: it's a discipline for finding the best move in any position, not just sharp ones.

5. Removing the defender

Removing the defender means attacking — or capturing — whatever piece is standing between you and a bigger prize, whether that's a mating square or a piece you want to win. If a checkmate or a winning capture is one defender away from working, look for a forcing move that eliminates that defender instead of abandoning the idea.

Watch out

Don't abandon a good idea just because one defender is in the way. Ask whether you can attack that defender first — with a check, a pawn push, or a second attacker — before giving up on the combination entirely.

6. Zwischenzug: the in-between move

A zwischenzug ("in-between move") is a forcing move you insert before playing the move that looked automatic. It has to demand a response — usually a check, or an attack on a queen or rook — so your opponent can't just ignore it and carry out their own plan.

Below, White's bishop on b5 is attacked by a bishop on a6, and a knight has just landed on d4. The automatic move is to recapture the knight with the c3-pawn — but Black would then answer ...Bxb5, and the trade is completely even. Inserting a check first changes the outcome entirely.

A zwischenzug doesn't have to be check — an attack on a queen or another major piece works just as well, since the opponent still can't stop to deal with anything else.

7. Discovered attacks

A discovered attack happens when moving one of your pieces uncovers an attack from a piece behind it. The strongest versions create two threats simultaneously: the piece that moves makes its own threat, while the piece it unmasks makes a second, unrelated one — and the opponent usually can't answer both.

The most dangerous discoveries combine a check with an attack on the opponent's queen, since the queen can't be defended by simply interposing on the checking line.

8. Sacrifice, deflection, and attraction

A sacrifice is a deliberate investment of material for an attack, initiative, or a forced win later — think of it as a middlegame cousin of the opening gambits from Chapter Twelve. The pay-off doesn't have to be immediate, but it should be calculated, not hopeful.

Deflection forces a defending piece away from a square or a piece it was protecting, usually with a bigger threat it can't decline. Attraction is the mirror image: instead of removing a defender, you lure — usually sacrifice — a piece onto a specific square where it becomes part of your mating net.

A rook sacrifice can work the same way through attraction: give up material specifically to force the enemy king onto a square where your remaining pieces can finish the job. 9. Chapter summary

CCA — checks, captures, attacks — is the discipline that turns tactical calculation from guesswork into a repeatable process: scan forcing moves first, for both sides, in that order, because forcing moves are what make a position calculable at all. This applies whether you're hunting a forced mate or simply looking for the best practical move in an equal position.

Removing the defender clears the one piece standing between you and a winning idea. A zwischenzug inserts a forcing move — a check or an attack on something valuable — before the move that looks automatic, often flipping an even trade into a material win. Discovered attacks let one move create two threats at once, especially dangerous when they combine a check with an attack on the opponent's queen. Sacrifices trade material for initiative or a forced follow-up; deflection and attraction both work by forcing an enemy piece onto (or away from) a specific square, whether by threat or by outright bait.

None of this replaces calculation, and none of it substitutes for practice. Pattern recognition — the whole foundation of this chapter — only comes from solving large numbers of puzzles until these shapes become instantly familiar.

Check yourself

What does CCA stand for, and why does the order matter?

Checks, captures, attacks — the categories of forcing moves to scan for on every move, for both yourself and your opponent. The order is a practical habit: checks are the most restrictive (often leaving only one legal reply), so evaluating them first narrows the position fastest before you spend time on quieter options.

Why are forcing moves easier to calculate than quiet ones?

A forcing move — a check, a capture, or a serious attack on a valuable piece — sharply limits the opponent's legal or sensible replies, sometimes to just one. A quiet move might allow thirty different responses, far too many to calculate through to a clear conclusion.

What is "removing the defender," and how do you find the right move for it?

It's attacking or capturing the piece that stands between you and a winning idea — a mating square or material you want to win. Find it by asking: if that one defender weren't there, what would I be threatening? Then look for a forcing move (often a pawn push or a capture) that removes or distracts it.

What makes a move a genuine zwischenzug rather than just an extra move?

It has to be forcing — typically a check or an attack on a queen or rook — so the opponent can't simply ignore it and play the move they were planning. An in-between move that doesn't demand a response just wastes a tempo instead of gaining one.

What is a discovered attack, and why are discovered checks on the queen especially strong?

It's when moving one piece reveals an attack from a piece that was behind it, so the moving piece and the revealed piece can threaten two different things at once. When the revealed threat is check, the opponent must deal with their king before doing anything else — including saving a queen that's also suddenly attacked, since a queen usually can't be defended just by blocking a check on a different line.

How is a sacrifice different from just blundering a piece?

A sacrifice is a deliberate, calculated investment of material made because it buys something concretely worth more — a forced mate, a decisive attack, or a won position — not a hope that something might turn up. The difference is the calculation behind it, not the fact that material is given up.

What's the difference between deflection and attraction?

Deflection forces a piece away from a square or piece it was defending, usually by threatening it with something bigger it can't ignore. Attraction does the opposite job with a similar method: it lures (often sacrifices material to force) an enemy piece onto a specific square, where it then becomes a liability in your mating net.

You're up material but your opponent threatens mate next move. What should guide your move choice?

Every candidate move needs to either remove the mating threat directly or answer it with a check or bigger threat of your own — quiet developing or consolidating moves aren't safe options here. Check each of your available checks and captures in turn, since one of them may force a favorable sequence rather than just surviving passively.
