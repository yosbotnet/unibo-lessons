1. What is a gambit

A gambit is a deliberate sacrifice of material, almost always a pawn, in exchange for a positional edge — faster development, extra central control, or an exposed enemy king — and occasionally for an outright quick checkmate. Gambits can be accepted (the material is taken) or declined (it is left alone), and the two choices often lead to completely different games.

Not every gambit carries the same risk. Some are "gambits" only in name, giving up little real danger to either side. Others are extremely sharp: if the tricks behind them do not work — because the opponent knows how to defend — the gambit player can end up simply down material with nothing to show for it. Because of that risk profile, gambits work best as an occasional weapon rather than the backbone of your entire repertoire.

2. Gambits for White: the King's and Danish Gambits

Roughly 90 percent of White gambits begin with 1.e4, since it is the most "open" first move, freeing both the queen and a bishop at once. Against the symmetrical reply 1...e5, one of the oldest and most famous tries is the King's Gambit, 2.f4:

Note

The King's Gambit dominated the 19th-century "Romantic era" of chess, when flashy material sacrifices for fast attacks were prized above caution. Engines have since shown that accurate defense equalizes for Black — but at club level, accurate defense is rare, which keeps this gambit dangerous in practice.

A second White try in symmetrical e4-e5 positions attacks from the other direction: the Danish Gambit, offering not one but two pawns for a devastating pair of bishops.

Key idea

In sharp gambits like the Danish, material is temporary and development is not: every extra pawn Black grabs costs a full move, while White's pieces come out with each recapture skipped. Reported online results put White's win rate here at around 60 percent — the highest of any common White club-level gambit.

3. Gambits for White: the Smith-Morra and the Queen's Gambit

Against the Sicilian Defense (1...c5), White has a gambit option too: 2.d4, offering the same kind of rapid development for a pawn. If Black accepts, 3.c3 offers a second pawn to open the position further; if that is also taken, White recaptures with the knight. This is the Smith-Morra Gambit:

Not every gambit is genuinely risky, though. The Queen's Gambit, 1.d4 d5 2.c4, is barely a gambit in practice: it is one of the most popular moves in the entire position, and if Black accepts with 2...dxc4, White simply continues with 3.e4, seizing the full center while the light-squared bishop already eyes the pawn on c4.

The Queen's Gambit has survived centuries of scrutiny, including the arrival of chess engines, without being refuted — proof that a "gambit" can also just be a sound, long-term opening choice.

4. Gambits for Black: the Blackburne Shilling Gambit

Gambits are riskier to play with Black than with White: moving second is already a small disadvantage, and adding a material deficit on top of it raises the stakes further. Black gambits tend to be all-or-nothing — they either win quickly or leave Black in real trouble. The first example, the Blackburne Shilling Gambit, is a pure trap out of the Italian Game.

Watch out

This trap only works if White walks into every step. The correct response, simply capturing the offered knight on move four, refutes the whole idea and leaves Black clearly worse. Treat it as an occasional surprise weapon at faster time controls, not a sound opening.

Even world-class players have tried the Englund at the board: in 2019, a grandmaster played it in a serious tournament game and lost — but the game dragged on for 114 moves, showing the resulting position was far from hopeless. Like the Blackburne Shilling Gambit, this line depends on White choosing the natural-looking moves rather than the objectively best ones (here, 6.Nc3 instead of 6.Bc3 avoids the whole trap).

Both Black gambits in this chapter share the same lesson: they can knock out an unprepared opponent in seven or eight moves, but a well-prepared White player who finds the accurate response early enough refutes them and leaves Black in a difficult position. Use them sparingly and know the refutation lines yourself, since you may face these same tricks as White.

6. Chapter summary

A gambit trades material, usually a pawn, for development, central control, or attacking chances, and can be accepted or declined by the opponent — so a gambit player still needs a plan for a "normal" game if the offer is refused. With White, gambits such as the King's, Danish, and Smith-Morra accelerate development and central control, usually earning the pawn back or more through a lasting initiative; the Queen's Gambit is barely a gambit at all and stands on its own as a sound, long-standing main opening. With Black, the risk-to-reward balance is steeper: tricks like the Blackburne Shilling and Englund Gambits can deliver forced mates in seven or eight moves against natural-looking play, but fail outright against an opponent who finds the best defense. Sprinkle gambits into your repertoire for variety and surprise value, not as your only way of playing chess.

Check yourself

What is a gambit, and what are the two ways an opponent can respond to one?

A gambit is a deliberate sacrifice of material, usually a pawn, in exchange for a positional or attacking advantage. The opponent can accept it (take the material) or decline it (leave it alone), and each choice leads to a different kind of game.

In the King's Gambit, what does White get in exchange for the f-pawn?

An open f-file and an open diagonal toward Black's king, plus faster development — for example, 3.Nf3 both develops a piece and stops the annoying ...Qh4+ check.

Why does White deliberately avoid recapturing pawns for several moves in the Danish Gambit?

Every pawn Black spends time capturing is a move Black is not using to develop. White uses those extra tempi to bring out both bishops actively, only recapturing (with Bxb2) once the second bishop is actually needed on that square.

What is the Smith-Morra Gambit, and what does White get for the pawn?

It arises after 1.e4 c5 2.d4 cxd4 3.c3 dxc3 4.Nxc3 against the Sicilian Defense. White gives up a pawn for open lines, faster development, and a lead in space, typically reaching a comfortable position with an easy lead in piece activity.

Why is the Queen's Gambit described as "barely a gambit" at all?

The gambit move, 2.c4, is simply the most popular and natural continuation in that position rather than a genuine risk. If Black accepts with 2...dxc4, White is not in danger — 3.e4 claims the full center and Black's extra pawn on c4 is more of a liability than a prize.

In the Blackburne Shilling Gambit, why is 4.Nxe5 a mistake for White, and what is the correct response instead?

Nxe5 grabs a pawn but walks into a forced sequence that ends in checkmate on Black's seventh move. The correct response to 3...Nd4 is simply 4.Nxd4, capturing the offered knight, which leaves White clearly better with no risk.

In the final mating position of the Blackburne Shilling Gambit, why can't White's bishop capture the checkmating knight on f3?

The bishop on e2 is pinned along the e-file: it sits between the Black queen on e4 and the White king on e1. Moving it to capture the knight would expose the king to the queen's check, which is illegal.

In the Englund Gambit trap, which White move opens the door to the forced mate, and what should White play instead?

6.Bc3, attacking the Black queen, allows 6...Bb4 pinning the bishop and leads to mate a few moves later. The safer move is 6.Nc3, which defends without walking into the pin.
