1. What counts as an endgame

There's no strict rule, but a useful working definition: an endgame is a position where at least half of the fifteen non-king pieces (two knights, two bishops, two rooks, one queen, per side counted together) have already left the board, often alongside several pawns. Once you're down to a handful of pieces, the game enters a different phase with its own rules of thumb.

Some endgames are solved: with perfect play from both sides, the result — win, loss, or draw — is known in advance, regardless of how "good" or "bad" either side's pieces look. King and pawn versus king is the classic example: there's no such thing as a slightly-better king and pawn ending, only a winning one, a drawing one, or a losing one. Other endgames are merely practical — not theoretically forced, but winnable with the right technique and enough pressure. Magnus Carlsen is famous for grinding out exactly this kind of ending, wearing opponents down over many hours in positions that "should" hold.

Six factors decide most endgames: material, activity (how well your pieces work, especially the king), weaknesses, zugzwang (being forced to move when every move makes things worse), domination (immobilizing an enemy piece), and passed pawns. The rest of this chapter walks through each piece combination and shows these factors at work.

Note

Chess vocabulary is a patchwork of borrowed words: zugzwang and zwischenzug are German, fianchetto and tempo are Italian, en passant and en prise are French.

2. King and pawn endgames

Pawn endings are the foundation everything else builds on, because so many other endgames get simplified into one. The first idea to learn is the square of the pawn: draw a square from the pawn to its promotion square, with sides equal to the number of moves the pawn needs to queen. If the defending king can step inside that square on its move, it catches the pawn; if not, the pawn queens untouched — no other pieces required.

Once both kings reach the pawn, everything hinges on whether the pawn stands in front of its own king or the king is already in front of its pawn. If the pawn is in front, the position is a fortress draw: the defending king just keeps stepping back into its shadow, mirroring every advance.

Flip the setup around — attacking king already in front of its pawn — and the same standoff now favors the attacker. That's where opposition and outflanking come in: whoever is forced to give up the face-off has to step aside, and the other king doesn't barge straight ahead but slides to the far side, taking space the defender can't contest.

Watch out

This outflanking technique only works with the pawn on the b through g files. A rook pawn (a- or h-file) has no room on the far side to outflank into: with the defending king able to reach the corner in front of it, the game is a draw no matter how well the attacker plays.

With more pawns on the board, an outside passed pawn — one on a file far from the rest of the pawns, that no enemy pawn can stop — becomes a decoy. The defender's king has to run over and deal with it, and by the time it gets there the attacking king has already infiltrated the other side of the board.

3. Knight endgames

King and knight can never checkmate a lone king, so once all the pawns disappear from a knight ending, it's automatically a draw. Knight endings otherwise behave a lot like pawn endings: an outside passed pawn is still the decisive weapon, because it creates two weaknesses at once — the defender's own pawn cluster, and the runaway passer — and a single king plus knight cannot guard both.

This idea decided a real game between Michael Adams and Jonathan Speelman (1995): a theoretically drawn knight ending slowly slid downhill once White's pawns became targets on both wings and Black couldn't defend everything at once.

4. Knight vs. bishop endgames

Bishops cover the whole board in a couple of moves; knights need several hops to change sides. That speed difference means a bishop can sometimes dominate a knight, leaving it with nowhere useful to go.

The reverse can also happen. If most pawns — attacker's and defender's — sit on the same color as the bishop, that bishop becomes a "bad" piece: it has no targets and can't get past its own pawn chain, while the knight jumps over everything.

As a rule, though, the bishop is the stronger piece in most endgames — it's faster over long distances and works well with pawns on both wings, a point revisited in Chapter Fifteen.

5. Bishop endgames

Bishop endings split into two very different categories depending on whether the bishops are the same color or opposite colors.

Opposite-colored bishops are famous for their drawing tendencies. A single extra pawn is routinely not enough to win, because the defending bishop can plant itself on a square of its own color directly in the pawn's path — or simply give itself up for the pawn — and a lone king and bishop can never deliver checkmate on their own.

What does break the fortress is scattered pawns on both sides of the board: the defending king and bishop can't rush to two different wings at once, so a second, distant passed pawn tends to win even against opposite bishops.

Same-colored bishop endings, by contrast, are much closer to king and pawn endings in feel — king activity and pawn placement decide everything. If one side's pawns are fixed on the color of their own bishop, that bishop can end up tied down defending them, and the position can collapse into zugzwang: any move makes things worse.

Magnus Carlsen demonstrated the technique against Alireza Firouzja in 2021: starting from a materially and structurally symmetrical bishop ending, Carlsen slowly herded his own pawns onto the color his bishop didn't need to defend, froze Black's pawns on the opposite color, then sacrificed a pawn to activate his king.

6. Rook endgames

Rook endings are the most common — and, by reputation, the most error-prone — endgames in chess; entire books are devoted to them. The basic winning plan when one side has an extra pawn is familiar: push the pawn, but also use the rook to cut off the enemy king along a file or rank, since a rook can deny access to dozens of squares at once in a way bishops and knights cannot.

Rook and pawn versus rook is not automatically winning, though. The key defensive resource is the Philidor Position: the defending rook sits on the rank just in front of the pawn, blocking the attacking king from crossing it. The moment the pawn advances onto that rank, the rook's own view along it is blocked, so it drops behind to start giving checks instead.

Rule of thumb

A grandmaster would agree to a draw in a Philidor-type position even three pawns to two, as long as the pawns are all on the same side of the board and the defender knows the technique. What actually wins rook endings is a pawn majority on the other wing from where the kings are — one side can't defend the loose pawn and stop the advancing one at the same time.

7. Queen endgames

Queen versus lone pawn is a fully solved endgame and, with one narrow exception, always wins for the side with the queen. The basic method: get the queen in front of the pawn so it can't advance, then walk your own king up the board to help capture it and finish with the king-and-queen checkmate technique from Chapter Four.

When the pawn is one square from queening and defended by its own king, the technique still wins on the b, d, e, and g files — a forced series of checks drives the defending king in front of its own pawn.

But on the a and h files, that same checking method runs out of room: the attacker can still force the king in front of the pawn, but capturing it — or improving further — walks straight into stalemate.

Queen endgames with pawns on both sides play very differently from other endgames: material imbalances matter less, because a queen alone can generate serious threats, and kings are usually kept tucked away rather than centralized — an exposed king invites perpetual check.

8. Chapter summary

Endgame evaluation always comes back to the same short list: material, activity, weaknesses, zugzwang, domination, and passed pawns. Pawn endings are the base case everything else reduces to — know the square of the pawn, the opposition, and outflanking, and you can convert or hold a huge share of positions. Bishops generally outperform knights in the endgame thanks to their speed, though a knight can dominate a "bad" bishop stuck behind its own pawns on the wrong color. Opposite-colored bishop endings are drawish even a pawn or two down; same-colored bishop endings hinge on activity and zugzwang like pawn endings do. Rook endings are the most complex and most common — cutting off the enemy king is the attacker's main tool, and the Philidor Position is the defender's. Queen versus pawn is winning except in specific stalemate traps on the edge files, and queen-and-pawn endings turn on king safety and perpetual check as much as on material.

Check yourself

What does it mean for an endgame to be "solved," and how is that different from a "practical" endgame?

A solved endgame has a known result — win, draw, or loss — with best play from both sides; king and pawn versus king is a classic example. A practical endgame isn't forced in that sense, but is winnable with the right technique and pressure; the defender might hold with perfect play, but real opponents often don't find it.

How do you find the square of a passed pawn, and what does it tell you?

Count how many moves the pawn needs to reach the promotion square, and draw a square of that many files and ranks starting from the pawn's square, extending toward its own promotion square. If the defending king can step inside that square on its move, it catches the pawn unaided; if it's outside and it isn't the defender's move to get in, the pawn queens.

In king and pawn versus king, when is having the opposition good, and when does it not matter?

Opposition matters when the kings are close to the pawn's path: the side forced to move away from a face-off usually has to give ground, letting the other king advance or outflank. It stops mattering once one king is comfortably in front of its own pawn with room to outflank, or once the pawn is a rook pawn and the defender's king has already reached the queening corner.

Why is king and knight versus king always a draw, and how does that shape knight endgames generally?

A lone king and knight cannot force checkmate against a bare king, so if all the pawns get traded off in a knight ending, it's a draw regardless of any earlier piece activity. That's why the winning plan in knight endings is almost always about pawns — especially an outside passed pawn creating "two weaknesses" the defender can't cover with one king and one knight.

What makes a bishop "bad," and how can a knight exploit it?

A bishop is bad when its own side's pawns sit on its color, blocking its diagonals and giving it few useful squares. A knight can exploit this by hopping freely to both colors and both sides of the board while the bishop stays boxed in with nothing to attack, as in the White knight versus Black bishop example in this chapter, where every White pawn sits on a dark square out of the light-squared bishop's reach.

Why are opposite-colored bishop endgames so often draws, even a pawn or two down?

The defending bishop can sit on a square of its own color directly in a pawn's path, or sacrifice itself for it, and a king plus bishop alone cannot checkmate a bare king. Two connected extra pawns often still lose to one well-placed blockading bishop move — only pawns split across both wings tend to overwhelm the defense.

What is the Philidor Position, and why does the defending rook have to leave the rank it was guarding once the pawn advances onto it?

It's the standard drawing technique in rook and pawn versus rook: the defending rook sits on the rank just ahead of the pawn, stopping the attacking king from crossing it. Once the pawn itself reaches that rank, it blocks the rook's own view along the rank, so the rook can no longer control the far side — it must switch to checking the attacking king from behind instead, which holds the draw by repetition.

Is queen versus a single pawn always winning? What's the exception?

Almost always — the winning technique works on the b, d, e, and g files. On the a and h files (and in certain king positions with a c- or f-pawn), the same checking method runs the defending king into a stalemate instead, so those specific configurations are draws.

Why do queen endgames with pawns on both sides play differently from other endgame types?

A lone queen generates enough threats that material imbalances matter less than in other endgames, and an exposed king is a serious liability because the opponent's queen can often deliver a perpetual check. Keeping the king tucked away safely is frequently more important than centralizing it, unlike in most other endgame types.
