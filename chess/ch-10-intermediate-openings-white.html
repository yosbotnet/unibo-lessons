1. This is not an openings book

Whole books exist on single openings; a single chapter cannot replace that depth. What follows is a set of concrete, practical recommendations for White at the intermediate level, built from years of coaching experience — not the objectively "best" openings by engine evaluation, and not necessarily what will suit your personal style. Treat them as a starting framework: study them, test them in real games, and feel free to replace any of them once you find something that fits you better.

Chapter 9 laid out the underlying philosophy: choose one first move, learn the plans it leads to in real depth, and prefer positions that are sound but slightly off the most-studied path, since that gives your opponent less to draw on from memory. Everything below follows that same logic — nothing here is the theoretical main line at grandmaster level, and that is deliberate.

2. The London System

For 1.d4, the London System remains the practical recommendation already introduced in the last chapter. The lesson generalizes: even the London, the hardest White opening for Black to disrupt, still needs a prepared response at nearly every branch. Whichever opening you choose, study the critical replies move by move, then play, review your losses, and repeat.

3. The Vienna System

For 1.e4, the recommendation is 2.Nc3 after 1...e5 — the Vienna System — instead of the far more common 2.Nf3. e4 openings offer more variety of positions than the London and, with good preparation, real chances to be winning within the first ten moves; the tradeoff is that Black has six or seven independent replies to prepare for. The Vienna is worth learning specifically because it stays easy to understand move after move while still carrying real traps for an unprepared opponent — you do not need to memorize dozens of only-computer-approved moves to play it well.

4. The Alapin against the Sicilian

The Sicilian Defense (1...c5) is the second most common reply to 1.e4 and one of the most theoretically dense openings in chess — not recommended as a deep study project for beginners or intermediates, though your opponents will play it anyway. The practical recommendation against it is 2.c3, the Alapin Variation, preparing d4 next move to claim the full center:

The appeal of the Alapin is practical rather than theoretical: it steers the game away from the sharpest, most heavily analyzed main lines of the Sicilian, cutting out most of the study time an opponent may have invested in it. Note that the Vienna System and the Alapin cannot both apply in the same game — Black chooses either 1...e5 or 1...c5, never both — so you simply reach for whichever plan the position calls for.

5. Advance variations against the French and Caro-Kann

Black does not have to answer 1.e4 with 1...e5 or the Sicilian. Two other common defenses, the French (1...e6) and the Caro-Kann (1...c6), were introduced back in Chapter Three as building-block openings. Against both, the recommendation is the same idea: when Black challenges the center with ...d5, push the e-pawn forward instead of capturing.

Restricting Black's space this way makes development awkward and, at the intermediate level, tends to provoke rash attempts to break the blockade — exactly the kind of mistake a solid, well-developed White position can punish. Black does have resources to fight back against both advance variations, so study the critical replies rather than assuming the space advantage wins by itself.

6. Chapter summary

With 1.e4, be ready for every major Black defense — the symmetrical 1...e5, the Sicilian, the French, the Caro-Kann, the Scandinavian, and the Modern — and expect to invest real study time building that repertoire. The Vienna System and the Alapin Variation are the recommended weapons against Black's two most common tries (1...e5 and the Sicilian) because they lead to predictable, easy-to-understand positions. With 1.d4, the London System offers a near-identical setup game after game, which saves hours of study compared to sharper alternatives. None of this is gospel — these are one coach's recommendations, and if your own White openings are already winning you games, there is no need to change them.

Check yourself

What pawn structure and piece placement define a typical London System for White?

A pawn triangle on d4, e3, and c3, the dark-squared bishop developed to f4 (or retreated to g3) before it gets blocked in, both knights developed toward the center, the light-squared bishop on d3, and the king castled kingside.

Why is 1...e5 (the Englund Gambit) not a good way for Black to stop the London System?

It simply loses a pawn: White captures on e5 and develops naturally. With correct play the gambit just leaves Black down material, although White should still know the specific traps Black can spring from it.

Why is 2...c5 considered Black's most testing try against the London System, and how does White answer it?

It opens the queen's diagonal toward b2 and pressures the White center, threatening to dislodge the d4 pawn. White meets it with c3, reinforcing the center so that any capture on d4 can be recaptured.

What is the point of 3.f4 in the Vienna Gambit, and why can White recover material so easily afterward?

3.f4 offers a pawn to open lines and seize the initiative; after 3...exf4 4.e5 kicks the Black knight back to g8, leaving White far ahead in development. White can then win the f4 pawn back with d4, attacking it with the dark-squared bishop.

Against which Black first move can White actually use the Vienna System, and why not against others?

Only against 1...e5. The Vienna's plans (2.Nc3 and the follow-up ideas) are built specifically around that structure; they do not apply if Black answers 1.e4 with, for example, the Sicilian.

What is the idea behind the Alapin Variation (2.c3) against the Sicilian Defense?

It prepares 3.d4, giving White two central pawns and directly challenging Black's c5 pawn, while steering the game away from the heavily analyzed main lines of the Sicilian that an opponent may have specifically prepared.

In the Advance Variations of the French and Caro-Kann, why does White push the e-pawn instead of capturing on d5?

Pushing to e5 gains space and restricts Black's position, making development harder for Black and often provoking mistakes as Black tries to break the resulting blockade.

If you already have a working opening repertoire with White, should you switch to the recommendations in this chapter?

Not necessarily. These are one coach's practical recommendations based on his own experience, not the single correct choice — if your current openings are winning you games and suit your style, there is no need to change them.
