1. The board and coordinates

A chessboard is an 8×8 grid of alternating light and dark squares. However you set it up, the square in each player's bottom-right corner must be light. Squares are named by combining a file letter with a rank number: the eight vertical columns (files) are lettered a through h from White's left to right, and the eight horizontal rows (ranks) are numbered 1 through 8 from White's side to Black's. The file always comes first, so the square marked below is f6.

Each side begins with sixteen pieces — thirty-two in total — set up in perfect mirror symmetry. White occupies ranks 1 and 2, Black occupies ranks 7 and 8, and each side's pawns stand in front of its own back rank of major and minor pieces. The queens start on the d-file and the kings on the e-file for both colors, and White always moves first.

2. Meet the pieces

Each type of piece moves differently, and none of them (except the knight) may jump over another piece — friend or enemy — that stands in its way. Pieces are also given a rough point value, used only to compare material, never to literally score the game:

PieceValueHow it moves
Pawn1Straight ahead only; captures diagonally
Knight3L-shape; only piece that can jump over others
Bishop3Diagonally, any distance, stays on one color
Rook5Horizontally or vertically, any distance
Queen9Any straight line or diagonal, any distance
King—One square in any direction; cannot be captured

The queen is the most powerful piece: it can slide along any rank, file, or diagonal for as many empty squares as it likes. The board below shows just four of the directions a queen on d4 could travel in a single move — the same is true along every other rank, file, and diagonal that passes through d4.

The rook moves the same way as the queen but only along ranks and files, never diagonally. The bishop is the mirror image: diagonals only. Because each side has one bishop that starts on a light square and one that starts on a dark square, a bishop is permanently confined to squares of its starting color.

The knight moves in an L-shape — two squares along a rank or file, then one square to the side — and it is the only piece allowed to hop over any piece in its path. That is why a knight can spring into the game on the very first move, before any pawns have cleared a path for the other pieces.

3. Pawns: special rules

Pawns are worth 1 point and follow rules unlike any other piece. They move straight ahead one square at a time, except on their very first move, when each pawn may advance either one or two squares. After that first move, it's one square at a time only. Pawns capture diagonally rather than straight ahead — they can never capture a piece directly in front of them, and they can never move diagonally onto an empty square.

When a pawn reaches the far rank — the 8th rank for White, the 1st for Black — it promotes. The player must immediately turn it into a knight, bishop, rook, or queen. A queen is almost always the right choice, since it's the most powerful piece, though rare positions call for underpromoting to a knight or rook instead.

The strangest pawn rule is en passant ("in passing"). If an enemy pawn uses its two-square first move to land directly beside one of your pawns, you may capture it as though it had only moved one square — but only on your very next move. Miss that window and the right disappears for the rest of the game.

Board 5 — Black pushes the d-pawn two squares to d5, landing beside White's pawn on e5. Immediately afterward, White may play exd6, capturing as though Black's pawn had only moved to d6.

Watch out

En passant is only legal on the move directly after the two-square pawn push. If you play something else first, you permanently lose the chance to capture that pawn this way.

4. The king: check, checkmate, stalemate, castling

The king moves one square in any direction and, unlike every other piece, can never be captured — it also can never move to a square where an enemy piece could capture it. That would be an illegal move, not a losing one; no legal chess move ever puts your own king in danger.

When a piece attacks the king, that's check, and the player in check must respond immediately — by moving the king to safety, blocking the attack with another piece, or capturing the attacker. Any move that leaves the king in check is illegal.

Checkmate is check with no way out: the king cannot move to safety, no piece can block, and nothing can capture the attacker. Checkmate ends the game immediately and is the most common way a game of chess is won — the whole subject of the next chapter. Stalemate is different: the side to move is not in check but has no legal move at all. Rather than a loss, stalemate is a draw.

Castling is a special king-and-rook move: the king slides two squares toward one of its own rooks, and that rook jumps to the square the king passed over. It is the only move in chess that moves two pieces at once, and it must satisfy several conditions: neither the king nor that rook may have moved yet, no piece may stand between them, the king may not currently be in check, and the king may not pass through or land on a square attacked by an enemy piece.

Key idea

Castling does two jobs at once: it tucks the king away from the center where most fighting happens, and it brings a rook into a more active square. That's why it's a core opening goal, covered again in the next two chapters.

5. Chess notation

Algebraic notation records a move as the piece that moved plus its destination square. Pawns are the exception: they carry no letter, so "e4" simply means a pawn advanced to e4. Every other piece uses a capital letter — B for bishop, N for knight (K is reserved for king), R for rook, Q for queen, and K for king.

Board 9 — This sequence is written 1. d4 d5 2. Bf4 Nc6: move number, then White's move, then Black's move.

A few symbols add detail. An "x" marks a capture (Qxd2). A "+" marks check (Rh8+), and "#" marks checkmate (Rh8#). Promotion is written with "=" followed by the new piece (g8=Q). Castling short is O-O and castling long is O-O-O.

Occasionally two identical pieces could both legally move to the same square, so the destination alone is ambiguous. Notation then adds the departure file or rank: "Nfe6" means the knight on the f-file (not another knight that could also reach e6) makes the move, while "N2d3" means the knight standing on the second rank does.

6. Scoring and ratings

A game of chess ends in a win, a loss, or a draw. In tournament scoring a win is worth 1 point, a draw is worth ½ point to each player, and a loss is worth 0 — though some events have experimented with 3 points for a win and 1 for a draw, to make decisive results more rewarding.

Your results also feed into a rating called Elo, named after its creator, physics professor Arpad Elo, which estimates your playing strength relative to other players. You earn an official Elo rating in sanctioned over-the-board events and a rating (often unofficial) on chess websites. The lowest possible rating, online or over the board, is 100. The highest over-the-board rating ever recorded belongs to Magnus Carlsen, who reached 2882 in 2014; online ratings have no fixed ceiling, but the strongest players top out around 3300–3400.

Note

Ratings are not perfectly comparable across websites or federations — a rating of 1500 on one platform is not necessarily the same strength as 1500 elsewhere. FIDE, the International Chess Federation, is treated as the most authoritative Elo for over-the-board play worldwide.

7. How to practice

Reading about chess only goes so far — improvement comes from practice, and a few tools make up the modern chess learning ecosystem:

  • Playing games, online or in person, is the most important activity. Time controls range from bullet (a few minutes total per player) through blitz and rapid up to classical games lasting several hours.
  • Chess engines such as Stockfish evaluate positions and suggest the objectively best moves; modern engines play at an estimated strength around 3700 Elo, far beyond any human.
  • Game analysis — reviewing a finished game, ideally with a stronger player or an engine — is how you turn playing into learning. An engine finds your mistakes; a human can also explain why they happened and how to avoid repeating them.
  • Bots are weaker computer opponents built to feel more human than a full-strength engine, useful for lower-pressure practice.
  • Puzzles present a single position with a specific solution, training a particular skill. The simplest kind is "checkmate in one"; puzzles get longer and harder as you improve, sometimes requiring you to calculate several moves ahead for both sides.
Rule of thumb

No single activity is enough on its own. Play regularly, review your own games afterward, and mix in puzzles to sharpen pattern recognition — the three reinforce each other.

Check yourself

What are the file and rank of the square directly below h8?

h7. Files stay the same letter going straight down or up the board; only the rank number changes.

Why can a bishop never switch from light squares to dark squares?

A bishop only moves diagonally, and every diagonal move keeps a piece on squares of the same color. Since each bishop starts on one color, it can never reach the other.

A pawn on its starting square wants to move two squares forward, but an enemy piece sits directly in front of it on the square it would pass over. Is the two-square move legal?

No. A pawn's forward moves (one or two squares) are blocked by any piece in the way, just like any non-capturing move; pawns cannot jump over pieces on their path even on their first move.

A White pawn stands on b7. What are its promotion options if it advances to b8?

It can become a queen, rook, bishop, or knight, the player's choice. A queen is normally best, but underpromoting to a knight or rook is occasionally the stronger move.

Black just pushed a pawn from c7 to c5, passing beside a White pawn on b5. White does not capture en passant on the very next move. Can White still take en passant two moves later?

No. The right to capture en passant only exists on the move immediately following the two-square push; once White plays something else, that pawn can no longer be captured this way.

What's the difference between checkmate and stalemate?

In checkmate the king is in check and has no legal way out, so the checkmated side loses. In stalemate the side to move is not in check but has no legal move at all, which ends the game as a draw.

Can a player castle if their king has never moved but the rook they want to castle with was moved earlier and then moved back to its original square?

No. Castling requires that the king and that specific rook have never moved at any point in the game, even if the rook ends up back on its starting square.

What does the move "Nfe6" tell you that "Ne6" wouldn't?

It tells you which of two knights that could both legally reach e6 is the one moving — specifically the knight standing on the f-file. Plain "Ne6" would be ambiguous.
