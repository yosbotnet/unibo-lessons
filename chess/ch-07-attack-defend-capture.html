1. Piece vision: seeing versus attacking

Every piece "sees" a set of squares from wherever it stands — its own pieces, empty squares, and enemy pieces. Pieces see the most from the center of the board, which is exactly why central control matters so much in the opening: a queen in the middle can see up to 27 squares, while the same queen in a corner sees far fewer. A knight is at its strongest on a central square too, sometimes called an "Octopus Knight" when it reaches eight target squares at once; in a corner it can see as few as two.

Seeing a piece is not the same as attacking it. A bishop that can technically reach a pawn only "attacks" it if capturing would actually gain something — if recapturing would cost you more than you gained, you're not really threatening anything, you're just looking at it. And you can never attack an enemy piece of the exact same type and value with a piece of that same type: a rook "sees" an enemy rook it lines up against, but so does the enemy rook, and since it would be the opponent's move after you played into that square, you'd simply lose your rook for nothing.

3. Forks

A fork is a single piece attacking two or more enemy pieces at once. The opponent can only save one of them, so a real fork guarantees you win material — as long as the pieces you're forking are actually undefended or more valuable than your forking piece. Forks often arrive with check, since the opponent is then forced to deal with the king first.

Knights are especially dangerous forkers, because nothing else moves the way they do — a knight attacking a queen, rook, or king can never be attacked back by that same piece in return. The most devastating version, hitting three or more pieces including the king, is nicknamed a family fork.

Watch out

Not every multi-piece attack is a real fork. Before committing, ask: are these pieces actually undefended, or would capturing either of them just trade my piece away for nothing?

4. Pins

A pin immobilizes a piece because moving it would expose a more valuable piece behind it on the same rank, file, or diagonal. There are two kinds. A relative pin is one where the pinned piece could legally move, but doing so would lose material.

An absolute pin is one where moving the pinned piece would be an illegal move, because the piece behind it is the king itself.

Once a piece is pinned, look for ways to add pressure to it — bring another attacker to bear on the pinned piece, so the defender is forced to give it up or lose even more material trying to save it. Be careful with the reverse side of this coin, too: a piece you use to block a check or an attack can end up pinned to your own king, restricting exactly the piece you needed to be flexible.

5. Skewers

A skewer is the mirror image of a pin: you attack a high-value piece first, and when it's forced to move, you capture a lesser piece standing behind it on the same line. Skewers most often target the king, since it must move out of check no matter what's behind it.

6. Discovered attacks and discovered checks

A discovered attack happens when you move one of your pieces out of the way, revealing an attack from a different piece that was blocked behind it. The moving piece can create a second threat of its own at the same time, putting the opponent in a bind — whichever threat they answer, the other one lands.

A discovered check is the same idea, except the revealed attack lands on the enemy king. It's one of the most dangerous tactics in chess, because the moving piece is completely free to do whatever it wants elsewhere on the board — the opponent still has to deal with the check first.

Key idea

When you're calculating a move, always ask what happens behind the piece you're moving, not just where it's going. The most damaging tactics in this chapter — forks aside — all come from paying attention to lines your own pieces open up, not just the squares they land on.

7. Summary

Every piece sees a mix of friendly pieces, enemy pieces, and empty squares, and central squares maximize that vision — but seeing a piece only matters if capturing it would actually gain something. When one of your pieces is attacked, you can move it, capture the attacker, protect it, or block the attack, and the right choice depends on whether the resulting trade favors you. Tactics turn these ideas into forcing combinations: forks hit two or more pieces at once (knights are especially good at this); pins immobilize a piece in front of something more valuable, whether that's a relative pin (costly but legal to break) or an absolute pin (illegal to break, because the king is behind it); skewers force a valuable piece to move and grab whatever's hiding behind it; and discovered attacks — including the especially dangerous discovered check — reveal a hidden threat the moment another piece steps out of the way.

Check yourself

Why is "seeing" a piece not the same as "attacking" it?

A piece only genuinely attacks another piece if capturing it would actually gain material. If the target is defended by something worth less than what you'd lose, you can see it, but you're not threatening anything real.

List the four ways to defend a piece under attack.

Move it (ideally somewhere that also creates a threat), capture the attacking piece, protect it with another piece, or block the line of attack — especially with a piece that also attacks back.

What makes knights especially dangerous forking pieces?

Nothing else moves the way a knight does, so a piece it attacks can never attack the knight back in the same way — a queen or rook forked by a knight has no way to simultaneously threaten it in return.

What's the difference between a relative pin and an absolute pin?

In a relative pin, moving the pinned piece is legal but loses material because a more valuable piece sits behind it. In an absolute pin, moving the pinned piece is illegal outright, because the piece behind it is the king.

How is a skewer different from a pin?

A pin attacks a lesser piece standing in front of a more valuable one, trapping the weaker piece in place. A skewer attacks the more valuable piece first; once it's forced to move away, you capture the lesser piece that was standing behind it.

What is a discovered check, and why is it so powerful?

It's a discovered attack where the revealed line hits the enemy king. It's powerful because the piece that moves to create it is completely free to do anything useful — capture a piece, attack another piece, whatever you like — since the opponent is forced to respond to the check regardless.

In Board 12, why can't Black simply save both the queen and the rook?

Because Bc5 creates two separate threats on one move: the bishop itself attacks the rook on f8, while moving off the e-file uncovers the rook on e1's attack on the queen on e6. Black only gets one move to answer both.

You've pinned an enemy knight to its queen with your bishop. What should you do next to press the advantage?

Add more pressure to the pinned knight by bringing another attacker to bear on it, forcing the defender to either lose the knight outright or give up more material trying to save it.
