1. Welcome to Part Two

Part One covered the mechanics of the game: how it ends, basic openings, basic endgames, tactics, and beginner strategy. Part Two builds on that foundation for players roughly in the 800–1200 rating range (still useful reading below or above that band). Across the intermediate chapters you will:

  • pick stronger openings for both colors so you reach the middlegame with an edge;
  • handle the harder endgames that beginner material skips;
  • recognize tactical patterns that produce multi-move combinations;
  • judge a position accurately instead of guessing;
  • build your own middlegame plans and disrupt your opponent's.

This chapter starts with openings, because the opening choices you settle on now shape how much of the rest of the game you get to play on your own terms. None of the material in Part Two requires you to have mastered Part One first, but it does assume you are comfortable with the basics: how games end, the golden opening moves, basic mates, and the elementary tactics already covered.

2. What goes into choosing an opening

There is no single "correct" opening. What suits you depends on your personality, how much you enjoy memorizing lines, how much study time you actually have, and your natural style — solid and principled versus sharp and attacking. Nobody else can make that choice for you, but a few criteria hold for everyone:

Key idea

Play the same opening repeatedly rather than switching every game. Repetition is what turns an opening from a memorized recipe into real understanding: you start recognizing your opponents' good and bad tries against it, and the resulting positions become familiar territory instead of a fresh puzzle each time.

That single habit — pick one plan per color and stick with it — does more for your rating than any specific opening choice.

3. Learning openings with White

With White you get to choose the first move, so pick one that actually leads somewhere and commit to it. A random flank push is "consistent" too, but it teaches you nothing useful. Two first moves cover almost everyone's needs: 1.d4 and 1.e4.

1.d4 can lead into the London System, the Queen's Gambit, the Trompowsky, and more. Its practical appeal is that one setup — the London — can be aimed at almost anything Black tries:

Because the London reaches similar structures move after move, you end up far better rehearsed in it than the opponent sitting across from you, who only meets it occasionally.

1.e4 is different: there is no one setup that answers everything. Black's two most common replies force very different middlegames, so an e4 player needs at least two plans ready.

Add the Caro-Kann, the French, the Modern, the Pirc, and the Scandinavian to the list of serious tries against 1.e4, and it is clear why e4 asks for more homework than d4. The reward is a wider variety of positions and, at club level, some very high early-win-rate lines for White — as long as you actually put in the study for each branch.

4. Learning openings with Black

Playing Black is structurally harder to prepare for, because White chooses the first move and you must have an answer ready for whatever that is. It sounds worse than it is: the overwhelming majority of games open with a small handful of White tries. Learn responses to those and you have covered almost every game you'll face — and remember, if White wastes moves in the opening, the golden-moves ideas from Chapter One let you seize the advantage immediately.

5. Main lines, popularity, and finding an edge

At grandmaster level, "main line" openings run extraordinarily deep — one well-known position in the Najdorf Sicilian is reached after 41 moves of known theory, and it has occurred hundreds of times in top-level games. You are extremely unlikely to need or benefit from memorizing that far. What works for a 2700-rated grandmaster is rarely what wins games for a club or intermediate player.

None of this means main lines are wrong to learn eventually. It means that, for now, an opening that scores well but is not the most popular choice at the top can be a genuine practical weapon: your opponent has to solve real problems over the board instead of reproducing memorized moves.

6. Chapter summary

Choose openings for both colors that fit your personality, memory, available study time, and preferred style — a thirty-minutes-a-day player and a tournament competitor should not necessarily study the same repertoire. With White, pick one first move and build depth in it; the London System is a low-maintenance option for 1.d4, while 1.e4 rewards more study with a wider range of positions. With Black, prepare for the handful of White replies you will actually face most often, or adopt a setup-based defense such as the King's Indian / Pirc to cut down the workload. In both cases, it is better to know a small number of openings deeply than to know a little about many. The next two chapters give concrete recommendations: one opening plan for White, then for Black.

Check yourself

Why is repeating the same opening more valuable than trying a different one every game?

Repetition builds real familiarity: you learn the resulting middlegame plans, recognize your opponents' good and bad tries, and stop treating every game as a fresh puzzle. Switching openings constantly means you never study any of them in depth.

Why is the London System recommended as a low-maintenance White opening?

Almost regardless of what Black plays, White can reach the same Bf4-plus-pawn-triangle structure, so the resulting positions stay familiar move after move. That means far less preparation than an opening where Black's reply changes the entire plan.

Why does 1.e4 need more preparation than 1.d4 for White?

There is no single London-style setup that works against every Black reply to 1.e4. Black's most common answers (1...e5 and the Sicilian 1...c5, plus the French, Caro-Kann, Modern, Pirc, and Scandinavian) lead to very different middlegames, so White needs a separate plan for each.

What is a "setup-based" Black opening, and what is its main downside?

It is a fixed arrangement of pawns and pieces — like the King's Indian / Pirc — that Black aims for regardless of White's first move, saving the need to prepare separately against every White try. The downside is reduced initiative: Black tends to react rather than fight for central space and the early attack.

In the King's Indian / Pirc setup, why is the pawn on d6 important?

It stops White from playing e5 and kicking the Black knight off f6, which would disrupt the whole setup before it is complete.

What does it mean that 2.Nc3 (the Vienna System) has been played "only" about 3 million times compared to 2.Nf3's 35 million?

It shows how quickly popularity drops once you move away from the most common choice. An opponent is far less likely to have studied deeply against the rarer move, even though it is a perfectly sound way to play.

Should you try to memorize 41 moves of Najdorf Sicilian theory like a grandmaster might?

No. That depth of preparation is built for top-level competition and is not a realistic or effective use of study time for a beginner or intermediate player; what wins games at that level is usually not what wins games for you.

You only have thirty minutes a day to study chess. Does that change what opening repertoire you should build?

Yes — with limited time, a smaller, simpler repertoire (for example, a single setup-based Black defense and the London System for White) is more realistic than trying to maintain deep, separate preparation against every possible reply.
