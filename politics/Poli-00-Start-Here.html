The course, at a glance

Foundations of Politics spends a semester on one question: who gets to decide how we live together, and why do the rest of us go along with it? Everything on the syllabus, states and constitutions, elections and parties, populists and global summits, is that one question wearing different clothes.

The practical shape of the course:

WhoProf. Giovanni Agostinis, Università di Bologna, Forlì campus, with a tutor for the exercise side.
Module 119 lectures in five blocks: what politics and political science are; the State, democracies and autocracies; the machinery of government; parties, voters, media, populism; the world beyond the State.
Module 2Seminars on one world region (Latin America, Asia, Europe, or Africa & the Middle East) ending in a group presentation on a country of your choice.
ReadingsChapters from friendly textbooks (Newton & Van Deth, the Caramani handbook, Heywood) plus a few short articles. All posted on Virtuale. No 900-page monsters.
The honest pitch

There is no math in this course. None. The skill it wants is one you already use daily: telling similar things apart and saying why. The difference between a democracy and a dressed-up autocracy, between a party and a lobby, between distrust and healthy doubt. Every lesson here turns those distinctions into stories you cannot unsee.

How you are graded

Attending students collect three scores, averaged:

  • Written test 1, mid-course: multiple choice plus open-ended questions on lectures 1 to 9.
  • Written test 2, end of module 1: same format, lectures 11 to 19.
  • Group presentation in the seminar module.

In the recent edition each written test ran 10 multiple-choice questions plus open questions worth 6 points each, graded with a published point-by-point grid (the format can shift a little year to year, so check the current announcements). Miss a test, or want a higher grade? There is a re-take at the end. Miss both tests and you sit the oral exam for non-attending students in the summer session, on the same syllabus.

The single most useful fact about this exam

The professor publishes correction guidelines after each test: the exact definition expected, the exact list of acceptable points, and how many points each is worth. That means the open questions are not essay-contest lotteries. They are checklists in disguise, and you can train for a checklist. The next section shows you how.

The answer recipe

Read three real open questions from this course's actual exams:

"Define what pressure groups are and present the determinants of their power. Answer in about 300 words."
"Identify the main differences between presidential and parliamentary systems and present (at least) one argument in favour of each. Answer in about 300 words."
"Define the Liberal International Order and discuss two challenges (one internal and one external) that undermine its stability."

Same skeleton every time: define X, then identify and discuss N things about X. And the grids score it the same way every time: about 2 points for a correct definition, and a fixed number of points for each item you not only name but discuss. So the recipe writes itself:

  1. Open with the textbook definition, one or two sentences, using the course's own vocabulary. That is the first 2 points banked in your first 40 words.
  2. Then one short paragraph per item: name it in bold-worthy words, explain it in one or two sentences, attach one concrete example. "Named + explained + example" is what graders read as discussed. A bare list gets half credit at best.
  3. Close with one sentence that answers the question's verb ("so the main difference is...", "so its stability is under pressure from both inside and outside"). Costs 20 words, signals control.

Do the word math: definition 40 words, three items at 70 words each, closing line 25. That is 275 words, which is why the questions say "about 300 words". The length is not a demand, it is a hint that the checklist has about three items.

"Political parties are organisations of politically like-minded people that seek political power and public office in order to realise their policies."
"A first democratic function is interest aggregation and articulation: parties collect the scattered demands of millions of citizens, bundle them into coherent programs, and give them a voice inside institutions. A pensioner and a student cannot each negotiate with parliament; a party turns their demands into a platform that can."
"A second function is guaranteeing the peaceful transfer of power. Because parties commit to the democratic rules of the game, the side that loses an election concedes and organises opposition instead of resistance, which is what makes alternation in government routine rather than a crisis."
"The 2020 transfer of power in the United States, contested by a candidate but completed by the institutions his own party staffed, shows both the function and its fragility."
"Parties, in short, are the machinery that connects citizens' demands to public decisions and keeps the competition for power inside peaceful rules."

The two ways good students lose points here

Listing without discussing. "Parties aggregate interests, mobilise voters, and manage conflict" names three functions in one breath and earns roughly one item's half credit. The grid pays per item discussed.
Answering a nearby question. Asked for functions "good for democracy", do not spend 150 words on party families or coalition types. Correct facts that ignore the question's verb are worth zero on this grid.

The multiple-choice game

The MCQs in this course are not trick arithmetic; they are look-alike pairs. Nearly every question shows you a definition or scenario and asks which of two neighboring concepts it is. The exam's favorite pairs, all trained in these lessons:

  • power vs authority (Poli-01)
  • totalitarian vs authoritarian (Poli-04)
  • democracy vs hybrid regime (Poli-03 and 04)
  • presidential vs semi-presidential (Poli-05)
  • pressure group vs social movement (Poli-09)
  • populism vs clientelism (Poli-11)
  • skepticism vs cynicism (Poli-13)

How to use these lessons

  1. Read in order, Poli-01 to Poli-13: the course builds its vocabulary brick by brick, and these pages reuse each brick on purpose so it sticks.
  2. Play every interactive and do every drill. The drills train exactly the look-alike pairs above; the feedback always ends with the sentence you would write in the exam.
  3. Do each lesson's quiz, then, when the whole course feels familiar, sit the Exam Room: a full written test with the real structure, including two open questions you grade against a grid, like the professor does.
  4. Keep the official slides and readings next to you. These lessons make them land; they do not replace them.
