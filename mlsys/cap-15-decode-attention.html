Part III — Attention · Chapter 15

Decode attention: split-K, FlashDecoding, KV-cache kernels

~70 min read1 interactive widget6 plateslab 14 · ~50 min on your GPU

The chapter in one paragraph

At decode, attention has one query row per head and a cache of T keys and values to read against it. There is no T2 matrix and almost no arithmetic: the operation is a matrix-vector product over the cache, half a FLOP per byte, and its only possible goal is to read the cache at full DRAM bandwidth. Chapter 14’s kernel cannot do that, for a reason chapter 2 taught: its parallelism was over query blocks, and a decode step has B·H query rows, often a dozen blocks for 48 SMs, far too few to keep the bytes in flight that Little’s law demands. The fix is to split the keys across blocks (split-K, which chapters 6 and 8 promised): every block produces a partial (m, l, Õ) for its slice of the cache, and chapter 14’s merge, which is associative, combines them. That is FlashDecoding. The chapter then goes down one level (how a warp reads a key row with 16-byte loads and reduces with shuffles, and how many loads it must keep in flight), looks at two ways of merging without a second kernel (the last-block pattern with atomics and __threadfence, and thread block clusters with distributed shared memory), and ends with the kernels that surround the cache: the append, the layouts, grouped-query attention that lets one cache read serve several heads, and the half- and quarter-size caches that Part IV will fill.

1. The shape, and the bytes

Chapter 10 named the two phases and chapter 13 costed them; recall only what this chapter builds on. After prefill, every layer holds a KV cache: the keys and values of all tokens so far, one [T, D] array of each per (sequence, head), written once and read at every later step. A decode step appends the new token’s key and value (section 7) and then, for each sequence b and head h, computes attention for one query row q against the T cached rows:

s[j] = q . k[j] / sqrt(D)      for j = 0 .. T-1        // a GEMV: [1, D] x [D, T]
p    = softmax(s)                                       // one row
o    = sum_j p[j] v[j]                                  // a GEMV: [1, T] x [T, D]

No mask: the new token sees everything. The scores are a row of T numbers, not a matrix, and the arithmetic is 4TD FLOPs for 2TD values read. In FP32 that is 0.5 FLOP per byte: chapter 6’s “linear M = 1” point on the roofline, a hundred times left of the ridge. And unlike the weights, the cache is private to its sequence (chapter 4): a bigger batch reads more caches, not the same one more often. So the kernel is a stream over the cache, and the whole design question is whether it streams at 590 GB/s or at a fraction of it.

The bytes, for the engine’s FP32 cache (D = 64, 256 bytes per key row, 512 per key with its value):

At batch 16 and 4K context the cache is ten times the weights, and attention is the decode step. Those are the times if the kernel streams. The next section is about why the obvious kernel does not.

2. Why one block per head starves the GPU

Take chapter 14’s design and set the query count to one: a thread block per (sequence, head) holding q in registers, streaming K and V tiles past it, with a running (m, l, Õ). It is correct, lab 14’s v2 is exactly that, and it is slow. The grid is B·H blocks: at batch 1 with GPT-2’s 12 heads, 12 blocks on 48 SMs. Thirty-six SMs idle, and that is only the first problem.

The second is chapter 3’s Little’s law. To sustain 672 GB/s at about 500 ns of DRAM latency, the GPU needs about 336 KB of requests in flight, 7 KB per SM, all the time. A block of 128 threads with one 16-byte load outstanding per thread has 2 KB in flight; with four keys unrolled and both K and V requested ahead, 16 KB (section 5). Twelve such blocks: 192 KB, 57% of what is needed, so the stream runs at about 57% of peak, from this one cause, before any other inefficiency. Put the two problems together in a model (it is the widget’s): bandwidth = min(peak, bytes in flight / latency), and bytes in flight = blocks × what each block keeps outstanding, capped by how many blocks the SMs can hold.

The model is crude (the per-SM load queues, the DRAM pattern and the tail of the last wave are not in it), but it says the right thing: the one-block-per-head kernel is bandwidth-starved exactly when the batch is small, which is when a user is waiting for each token. At batch 16 the same kernel is fine. Chapter 4 called this “attention does not batch”; here is the other side of it: attention does not parallelize either, unless you split something other than the queries.

Your move: a serving engine runs batch 64 with 32 heads: 2,048 blocks. Is one block per (sequence, head) still a problem, and what in section 1’s arithmetic becomes the problem instead?

Not for bandwidth: 2,048 blocks are 43 per SM, far more than the SMs hold at once, so the stream runs at peak and the only loss is the last wave’s tail. The problem is the bytes themselves: 64 private caches, each read once per step, with nothing shared between sequences. Fixing the kernel cannot help; only smaller caches can (FP16 and FP8 in section 7, and GQA in section 6, which lets one cache serve several heads).

3. Split-K: partials and the combine

The dot products of a GEMV sum over K (here, over the keys), and nothing requires one block to do the whole sum: chapter 8 named this split-K for matmuls with too few output tiles, and chapter 6 promised it for decode. Cut the T keys of a (sequence, head) into S chunks, give each chunk to a block, and the grid becomes B·H·S. The attention wrinkle is the softmax: a block that saw only its chunk cannot normalize. But you already know what to do, from chapter 14’s second Your move: each block produces a partial (m, l, Õ) for its chunk, and the merge rule combines partials in any order. That is FlashDecoding (Dao, Haziza, Massa, Sizov, 2023): FlashAttention’s loop with one more level of parallelism, over the key sequence, plus a reduction.

// FlashDecoding, one (b, h).  Kernel 1: grid (B*H, S), block s handles keys [s*chunk, (s+1)*chunk)
m_s, l_s, O~_s = online attention of q over the chunk        // chapter 14's loop, one row
write (m_s, l_s, O~_s) to scratch                            // D + 2 floats per block

// Kernel 2: grid B*H, one small block each
M = max_s m_s
L = sum_s l_s * exp(m_s - M)
O = sum_s O~_s * exp(m_s - M) / L

Here it is on one row with three splits and D = 1, continuing chapter 14’s toy (scores 1, 3 | 2, 5 | 4, 0 with values 10, 20 | 30, 40 | 50, 60; illustrative numbers):

The blocks never communicated, and the order of the rows in the table does not matter. The partials are small: D + 2 floats per block, 264 bytes at D = 64, against the chunk’s 512·T/S bytes of cache; with 256 keys per chunk, 0.2% of the traffic.

Choosing the number of splits

Two costs pull in opposite directions. More splits mean more blocks in flight, until the grid is a few blocks per SM and bandwidth is saturated; beyond that they add nothing. More splits also mean more partials to write and merge, and a chunk that is too short (a few dozen keys) spends its life in the block’s prologue and epilogue rather than streaming. So the rule in the lab and the engine is a clamp: enough splits to reach about four blocks per SM, never more than one per 256 keys, never fewer than one:

// lab 14 / engine: how many ways to split the keys of each (b, h)
int choose_splits(int B, int H, int T_ctx) {
    int want = (192 + B * H - 1) / (B * H);          // ~4 blocks per SM on 48 SMs
    int max_s = (T_ctx + 255) / 256;                  // at least 256 keys per chunk
    return max(1, min(want, max_s));
}

With B = 1 and 12 heads at T = 4,096: 16 splits of 256 keys, 192 blocks. At B = 16: one split, because 192 blocks already exist, and the combine kernel is not launched at all. At T = 256 and B = 1: one split, however starved, because there is nothing to cut.

Your move: at B = 1, 12 heads, T = 1,024, the rule gives 4 splits (48 blocks). The combine kernel costs a launch (a few microseconds) and the stream of 6.3 MB costs about 11 µs at full bandwidth. Is splitting still worth it here?

Barely, and it depends on the launch floor. Without splits, 12 blocks at the modelled 57% take about 19 µs; with four splits, 48 blocks reach close to full bandwidth for about 11 µs, plus 3–5 µs for the second kernel: a saving of a few microseconds out of twenty, and it vanishes if the kernels are not back to back in a CUDA graph. The short-context, small-batch corner is where the launch floor, not the bandwidth, decides; chapter 11 measured it. Section 4’s single-kernel combine exists for exactly this corner.

4. Combining without a second kernel

The combine is a second launch and a dependency: the engine cannot start the next GEMM until it has run. Two ways to fold it into the first kernel, one old and one new, and both need vocabulary this course has used only in passing.

Atomics, scopes and fences

An atomic operation is a read-modify-write of one memory word that no other thread can interleave with: atomicAdd(&x, 1) returns the old value and stores the new one as one indivisible step, performed by the L2 slice that owns the address (chapter 11 used them for the histogram of top-k sampling). Its scope says who it is indivisible with respect to: atomicAdd_block among the threads of the block, atomicAdd (the default) among all threads on the device, atomicAdd_system including the CPU. The atomic itself is not the hard part. The hard part is that ordinary stores are not ordered as seen from another block: a block that writes its partial and then increments a counter may have the increment become visible first, because the partial’s stores are still on their way to L2 while the atomic, which goes straight to L2, has already landed. A memory fence fixes the order: __threadfence() guarantees that every write this thread made before the fence is visible to every thread on the device before any write it makes after the fence. It has the same three scopes (__threadfence_block, __threadfence, __threadfence_system). A fence orders; it does not wait for anyone, and it does not make the data arrive sooner.

The last-block pattern

With those two tools the combine can be done by whichever block finishes last. Every block writes its partial, fences, and takes a ticket from a per-(b, h) counter; the block whose ticket is S − 1 knows all other partials are written and does the merge:

// lab 14, decode_v3b_lastblock: the tail of each block, after computing (m, l, o) for its chunk
__shared__ bool am_last;
publish(partials + (bh * S + split) * (D + 2), m, l, o);   // D + 2 floats, plain stores
__threadfence();                                           // my stores are visible device-wide before anything after this
__syncthreads();                                           // ...and every thread of the block has fenced
if (tid == 0) am_last = (atomicAdd(&counter[bh], 1u) == S - 1);
__syncthreads();
if (!am_last) return;
__threadfence();                                           // the others' partials are visible to me
// thread d < D merges the S partials; read them with __ldcg (L2, not a possibly stale L1)
float M = -INF;  for (s) M = fmaxf(M, __ldcg(&partials[(bh * S + s) * (D + 2)]));
float L = 0, O = 0;
for (s) { float ms = __ldcg(...m_s), ls = __ldcg(...l_s), os = __ldcg(...o_s[d]);
          float w = __expf(ms - M);  L += ls * w;  O += os * w; }
att[b * C + h * D + d] = O / L;
if (tid == 0) counter[bh] = 0;                             // reset for the next launch (plain store is fine: nobody races)

Every line answers a failure. Without the first fence, the last block can read a partial that is not there yet. Without the __syncthreads() before the atomic, thread 0 can take its ticket while a neighbour is still storing its part of Õ. Without __ldcg (or a volatile pointer, which the CUDA Programming Guide’s example uses), the reads may be served from an L1 that is per SM and never kept coherent; a block that has not touched those lines will miss its L1 anyway, but the habit is what keeps the next kernel correct. And the counter must be zero before the launch (a cudaMemset once, then the last block resets it). The cost is one atomic per block (an L2 round trip, a microsecond or so at the end of each block, not on its critical path) and the merge serialized into one block per (b, h), which is what the separate kernel did anyway. The saving is the launch and the dependency.

Thread block clusters and distributed shared memory

Hopper introduced a second tool. A thread block cluster is a group of up to 8 blocks (16 with an opt-in) that the hardware schedules together on one GPC (a group of SMs) and that can read and write each other’s shared memory, called distributed shared memory, and synchronize with a cluster-wide barrier. A kernel declares its cluster shape with __cluster_dims__(S, 1, 1) or at launch with cudaLaunchKernelEx; inside, cooperative_groups::this_cluster() gives cluster.sync() and cluster.map_shared_rank(ptr, rank), which turns a pointer into the block’s own shared memory into one that reaches block rank’s copy. For split-K that is the natural shape: the S splits of a (b, h) are one cluster, each block leaves its partial in its own shared memory, one cluster.sync(), and block 0 reads the other seven partials over the SM-to-SM network and writes the output; a second cluster.sync() keeps the others alive until it is done. No scratch buffer, no atomics, no fence, no second kernel.

Does your sm_120 have clusters?

The CUDA C++ Programming Guide’s feature table (CUDA 13, checked in October 2026) lists Thread Block Cluster and Distributed Shared Memory as supported for compute capabilities 9.0, 10.x, 11.0 and 12.x, which includes your card, and as unsupported on 7.x and 8.x. This has not been verified on an RTX 5070 in this course; the hardware is documented but the kernel has never run here, and forum reports suggest that the related TMA multicast across a cluster is absent on consumer parts. Lab 14’s v6_cluster is therefore optional: it compiles only for ARCH ≥ sm_90, asks the driver with cudaOccupancyMaxActiveClusters how many clusters of 8 can be resident, and skips itself with a message if the answer is zero. If it runs and passes, that is the verification; paste the line.

Your move: in the last-block kernel, a colleague moves the __threadfence() to after the atomicAdd, arguing that the fence should come as late as possible. What can now go wrong, and how often will it show?

The ticket can become visible before the partial: the last block reads a partial whose Õ is still in flight and merges garbage, or zeros from a previous launch. It will show rarely, because the stores usually land within the microsecond the atomic takes, and almost never on cudasim, which runs blocks one after another. That is the signature of a memory-ordering bug: a correct answer most of the time and a wrong one under load, on a different GPU, or after an unrelated change. The fence must sit between the data and the signal.

5. Inside a block: lanes over D, loads in flight

Sections 2–4 decided how many blocks there are. Inside a block, the question is chapter 3’s: do the warps read whole sectors, and do they keep enough requests outstanding? The organising choice is lanes across the head dimension: a key row is D = 64 floats, 256 contiguous bytes, and a float4 per lane covers it with 16 lanes. So a warp handles two keys per step: lanes 0–15 read key j, lanes 16–31 read key j+1, each lane one 16-byte load, the warp 512 contiguous bytes, every sector fully used. Each lane multiplies its four values of q (held in registers since the start) by its four of k and adds: a partial dot product. The full score is the sum over the 16 lanes of the half-warp, which is four __shfl_xor_sync steps (8, 4, 2, 1), after which every lane of the half-warp holds the score. The online update follows per key, with the half-warp’s running m and l duplicated in all 16 lanes, and Õ spread the same way as the loads: each lane owns four of the 64 output dimensions and does o4 += p · v4 with the four values of v it loaded.

// lab 14, decode_v2_online / v3: the inner loop of one warp (half = lane / 16, c = lane % 16)
for (int j = key0 + half; j < key_end; j += 2 * 4) {           // 4 warps x 2 keys per warp-step
    float4 k4 = *reinterpret_cast<const float4*>(kc + (size_t)j * D + 4 * c);   // 16 B per lane, 256 B per half-warp
    float4 v4 = *reinterpret_cast<const float4*>(vc + (size_t)j * D + 4 * c);
    float s = q4.x * k4.x + q4.y * k4.y + q4.z * k4.z + q4.w * k4.w;                // partial dot: 4 of 64 terms
    s += __shfl_xor_sync(FULL, s, 8); s += __shfl_xor_sync(FULL, s, 4);
    s += __shfl_xor_sync(FULL, s, 2); s += __shfl_xor_sync(FULL, s, 1);              // all 16 lanes hold the score
    s *= scale;
    float m_new = fmaxf(m, s), alpha = __expf(m - m_new), p = __expf(s - m_new);
    l = alpha * l + p;
    o4.x = alpha * o4.x + p * v4.x;  o4.y = alpha * o4.y + p * v4.y;  o4.z = ...;  o4.w = ...;
    m = m_new;
}
// then: the 8 half-warps of the block merge their (m, l, o4-slices) through shared memory; thread d writes o[d] / l

As written, each lane has one key’s K and V loads (32 bytes) outstanding before it needs them: 1 KB per warp, 4 KB per block, a quarter of the 16 KB section 2 assumed. The fix is chapter 3’s again, in the form chapter 8 used for the double buffer: issue the loads for several keys before using any of them. The lab’s kernels unroll four keys per half-warp (DEC_UNROLL = 4): eight float4 loads issued back to back, 128 bytes per lane in flight, then the four shuffle-reductions and updates. The compiler hoists the loads only if nothing between them can alias the stores, which is why the cache pointers are const __restrict__. With 16 KB per block and 192 blocks the grid keeps 3 MB in flight, ten times what Little’s law needs; the stream is then limited by DRAM, which is the goal.

Your move: switch the cache to FP16. A 16-byte load now holds 8 values, so a 64-wide key row takes 8 lanes. How does the warp’s step change, what happens to the shuffle tree, and what must not change?

Eight lanes per key, four keys per warp-step, and each lane’s slice of the output grows to 8 dimensions. The shuffle tree loses one level (xor 4, 2, 1: three steps over eight lanes). The loads must stay 16 bytes (uint4 or __half2 × 4) and the warp must still read a contiguous run: four keys × 128 bytes, 16 sectors, as before. What must not change is the arithmetic: the halves are converted to FP32 before the FMAs, and m, l, Õ stay FP32 (chapter 6’s accumulation lesson; a running sum of thousands of exponentials in FP16 loses digits). That is lab 14’s v4_half: half the bytes, nearly half the time.

6. Grouped-query attention: one read, several heads

Everything so far reads each cached key once per query head. GPT-2 has one key head per query head, so there is nothing to share. Modern models change that on purpose. Multi-query attention (MQA, Shazeer 2019) keeps H query heads but a single key head and value head; grouped-query attention (GQA, Ainslie et al. 2023) keeps Hkv key/value heads and lets a group of g = H/Hkv query heads share each one. Llama-3-8B has 32 query heads and 8 key/value heads: g = 4. The model was trained that way, so the sharing is exact, not an approximation, and it exists for this chapter’s reason: the cache shrinks by g (chapter 13 counted it), and the kernel reads each key once for the whole group.

The kernel change is small. A block (or a cluster of splits) now serves one (b, kv-head) and the g query rows of its group, held in shared memory or registers. Each key row loaded gives g dot products instead of one, each with its own softmax state and its own Õ slice per lane: g times the FMAs and the registers, the same bytes. Intensity goes from 0.5 to 0.5g FLOP/B in FP32, 2g in FP16; at g = 4 it is still far below the ridge, so the kernel stays memory-bound and gets faster by exactly the bytes it no longer reads. When the group is large (g = 8 or more) the g query rows against the chunk’s keys form a small matrix product with M = g, and production kernels put the group into the M dimension of an mma.sync so that the scores come from the tensor cores: FlashDecoding’s kernels and TensorRT-LLM’s XQA do this. For g = 4 on your card the FP32 lanes keep up, and lab 14’s v5_gqa stays scalar.

4 KB per token per layer × 8,192 × 32 = 1.07 GB per step, about 1.8 ms at 590 GB/s, against the 27 ms that reading the model’s 16 GB of FP16 weights would take if they fit in 12 GB (they do not: Part IV’s problem). Without GQA, 32 KV heads: 4.3 GB per step and 7.3 ms, a quarter of the weight time instead of a fifteenth. The group of 4 is worth 4× on the dominant term at long context, which is why every recent model uses it.

7. The kernels around the cache

The append

Before attention reads the cache, the new token’s k and v must be in it. In the engine that is the contract’s kv_append: for each (b, h), copy the 64 floats of k from the qkv row (columns C + h·D onward) to position p of k_cache[b][h], and v likewise. A pure copy, 2·B·C·4 bytes read and written per step, one thread per float, coalesced on both sides; at prefill it copies T tokens at once. Real engines do not run it as a kernel at all: the QKV GEMM’s epilogue writes k and v straight into the cache instead of into the qkv buffer (chapter 9’s epilogue fusion, with a different destination), and in models with rotary position embeddings the rotation of q and k happens in the same epilogue, before the write, so the cache holds rotated keys and the rotation is never redone (the Modern Transformer page covers what RoPE computes; here it is one more passenger of the epilogue).

Layouts

The contract’s cache is [B][H][T_max][D]: one head’s keys contiguous, which is what section 5’s streaming reads want, with Tmax slots reserved per sequence. That reservation is the layout’s cost: a sequence that stops at 300 tokens has paid for 1,024, and a new sequence cannot use the gap. The alternatives trade the read pattern against that waste:

Smaller numbers

Section 5’s Your move already built the FP16 cache: half the bytes, 16-byte loads of 8 values, FP32 arithmetic. The next step is FP8 (E4M3, chapter 6’s table): a quarter of the FP32 bytes, with a scale factor per block of values, typically one per key row per head or per 64 values, because 448 is the largest E4M3 number and keys are not naturally in that range. The kernel loads 16 bytes (16 values and, from a side array, their scale), converts to FP32, multiplies by the scale, and proceeds as before; the dot product, the softmax and Õ stay FP32. Chapter 16 is about where the scales come from and what the rounding costs in accuracy; the kernel side is what you just read: bytes shrink, arithmetic does not change, and the merge is untouched.

Check your understanding

Why is decode attention memory-bound at every batch size, when a linear layer at the same batch is not?

A linear layer’s weights are shared by every sequence in the batch, so a bigger batch reuses each byte more. Each sequence’s KV cache is private: a bigger batch reads more caches, each once. FLOPs and bytes grow together, and the ratio stays at about half a FLOP per byte in FP32.

A decode kernel with one block per (sequence, head) runs at a third of DRAM bandwidth at batch 1 and at full bandwidth at batch 8. What changed, in terms of Little’s law?

The number of blocks, and with it the bytes in flight. Bandwidth needs latency × bandwidth bytes outstanding (a few hundred KB); each block keeps a bounded amount outstanding, so too few blocks cannot cover it. At batch 8 the grid is eight times larger and the product exceeds what is needed.

What does each split-K block hand to the combine, and why does the combine not need to know the order the blocks ran in?

A partial (m, l, Õ): its chunk’s max, the sum of exponentials relative to that max, and the unnormalized output. The merge rescales each by exp(ms − M) and sums; max and weighted sums are associative and commutative, so any order gives the same result.

In the last-block pattern, what does __threadfence() guarantee, and what does it not?

It guarantees that the thread’s writes before the fence are visible to all threads on the device before any of its writes after it, so a partial written before the fence is visible before the counter increment after it. It does not wait for other threads, does not synchronize, and does not speed anything up; the __syncthreads() is still needed so that all threads’ partial writes precede thread 0’s atomic.

Why does the block that merges the partials read them with __ldcg or through a volatile pointer?

L1 caches are per SM and not kept coherent with writes from other SMs. A read that could be served from L1 might return a stale line; __ldcg caches only at L2, where the other blocks’ writes have landed after their fences.

What is distributed shared memory, and how does it remove the combine kernel?

In a thread block cluster, blocks scheduled together can address each other’s shared memory. Each split leaves its partial in its own shared memory; after a cluster-wide barrier one block reads the others’ partials directly and writes the output; a second barrier keeps the others resident until it is done. No scratch buffer, counter or second launch.

In the warp-level design, why does a lane load a float4 of a key row rather than a whole key row?

So that the 16 lanes of a half-warp read one contiguous 256-byte row with full sectors, and the warp two rows per instruction. One row per lane would make 32 lanes read 32 rows 256 bytes apart: the same bytes in 8× the sectors per instruction, and uncoalesced. The price is a 16-lane shuffle reduction per score.

With grouped-query attention of group size g, how do the bytes, the FLOPs and the bound of the decode kernel change?

Bytes per query head drop by g, because one key/value read serves g query heads; FLOPs per key row grow by g (one dot product and one update per query in the group); intensity rises by g. At the group sizes in use it stays below the ridge, so the kernel remains memory-bound and runs faster by about the bytes saved.
