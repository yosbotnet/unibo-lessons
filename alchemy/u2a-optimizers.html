Unit 2 · Optimization 1 · part a (CS 312 lecture 3, assignment 2)

Optimizers: noise, curvature and norms

~60 min read2 interactive widgets12 sealed predictions3 audit drills

The unit in one paragraph

An optimizer is four dials: a learning rate, a batch size, a preconditioner and momentum. Two simple models predict most of how they interact. The noisy quadratic treats the loss as a bowl with steep and flat directions and the gradient as a noisy measurement of its slope. It explains why the best learning rate grows with batch size and then stops growing, why larger batches eventually stop paying, and why momentum and preconditioning help mostly at large batch. The rotational picture of the weights starts from the fact that most weights in a normalized transformer only matter through their direction. Gradient steps then make their norms grow, weight decay pulls them back, and the two settle into an equilibrium where the learning rate and the weight decay act only through their product. That product explains measured results from CS 312 that look strange otherwise, such as why the right response to an eight times larger batch is eight times more weight decay. Hyperball fixes the norm by construction, so that the learning rate directly sets how far each weight turns. Twelve sealed predictions, most checked against CS 312’s measured d8 runs, and three audit drills.

1. What an optimizer is made of

Plain stochastic gradient descent takes one step downhill on a minibatch: θ ← θ − η ∇ℓ(θ). Every optimizer in use is that update with four things changed:

  • The learning rate η, and how it changes over training (the schedule).
  • The batch: how many examples are averaged into each gradient. More examples mean less noise but fewer steps for the same data.
  • A preconditioner A: θ ← θ − η A g. Think of it as a change of coordinates that gives each direction its own step size. It must be positive definite, with eigenvalues kept away from zero and infinity, or it stops being a descent method.
  • Momentum: a running sum of past gradients, so that directions where gradients keep agreeing get faster steps.

AdamW, the d8 default, uses all four. Elementwise, for a weight matrix W with gradient g at step t:

m ← β1 m + (1 − β1) g       v ← β2 v + (1 − β2) g2     momentum, and a running second moment
U = [m / (1 − β1t)] / ( √[v / (1 − β2t)] + ε )     bias-corrected; dividing by √v is a diagonal preconditioner
W ← W − η U − η λ W     the step, then decay applied to the weights directly

The “W” stands for decoupled weight decay (Loshchilov and Hutter). Adding λ |W|²/2 to the loss instead would send the decay through the preconditioner, dividing it by √v and shrinking each coordinate by a different amount. AdamW shrinks every weight by the same factor 1 − ηλ per step. Hold on to that factor: it is the subject of sections 6 to 8. The d8 recipe uses β = (0.9, 0.95), ε = 10−8, peak η = 0.003, λ = 0.1, and exempts embeddings, normalization gains and biases from decay.

Why deep-learning optimizers are judged differently

Classical optimization theory asks how fast a method reaches a precise stationary point, on every function in some class. Training a language model asks for neither. Nobody runs a model to convergence; the goal is a good loose solution within a fixed budget, on one narrow family of functions. Adam is the standard example. Its original convergence proof contained an error, and Reddi and colleagues later built simple problems on which it does not converge. Practice did not change, because the guarantee was never what anyone relied on. CS 312’s stance follows from this. Convex intuition is a source of hypotheses, not a verdict. Build top-down models of the large-scale behaviour (how a recipe responds to learning rate, batch, scale), then reconcile them with bottom-up mechanisms. The rest of this unit does exactly that, twice.

Your move: multiply the loss by 10. What happens to an SGD step, and to an Adam step?

SGD’s gradient, and therefore its step, gets 10 times larger: the same η is now a 10 times larger learning rate, enough to diverge. Adam divides by the running √v, which also grows 10 times, so its step barely changes. The only exception is ε, which becomes relatively smaller. Adam is nearly invariant to the scale of the gradient; its step size per coordinate is set by η. Keep this in mind for section 6: a step whose size ignores the gradient’s scale is what makes weight norms matter.

2. The noisy quadratic

Zhang and colleagues (2019) asked how far one can get by pretending the loss is a quadratic bowl and the gradient is its slope plus Gaussian noise. Further than you would expect: their noisy quadratic model (NQM) predicted how the best learning rate, the effect of momentum and the benefit of preconditioning change with batch size in real networks. Its appeal is that everything about it can be solved exactly.

Rotate coordinates so that the curvature matrix H is diagonal, with eigenvalues hi. Then the loss is ½ Σ hi θi2 and the minibatch gradient is Hθ + ε, with noise ε ~ N(0, C/B): averaging B independent examples divides the variance by B. If C is diagonal in the same coordinates, every coordinate evolves on its own. With SGD at step size α:

θi ← (1 − αhi) θi − α εi,    εi ~ N(0, ci/B)
E[ℓi(t)] = (1 − αhi)2t E[ℓi(0)]  +  [1 − (1 − αhi)2t] · αci / (2B(2 − αhi))

The expected loss has two parts derived. The first is what is left of the start, shrinking by a factor (1 − αh)² per step. The second is a noise floor the run settles on, proportional to α/B. Three consequences follow, and between them they hold most of this unit:

  1. The steepest direction sets the speed limit. The factor |1 − αh| must stay below 1 in every direction, so α < 2/hmax. Past that the steep direction overshoots by more each step and the run diverges.
  2. The flattest direction sets the duration. It shrinks at a rate of about αhmin per step, so it needs on the order of 1/(αhmin) steps, and α cannot be raised to hurry it past the limit set by the steep direction.
  3. The noise floor costs α/B. A larger learning rate finishes the flat direction sooner and parks the run higher. A larger batch lowers the floor, but at a fixed data budget it also means fewer steps.
Your move: derive the noise floor from the update rule.

Square the update and take expectations; the noise is independent of θ, so the cross term vanishes: v′ = (1 − αh)² v + α²c/B, where v = E[θ²]. At the fixed point v = α²c / (B(1 − (1 − αh)²)) = αc / (B h (2 − αh)). The loss is ½hv = αc / (2B(2 − αh)). It depends on α and B only through α/B as long as αh is small, which is the seed of the linear scaling rule in section 3.

CS 312’s assignment 2 uses the smallest interesting case: two coordinates with H = diag(1, 10), noise covariance I (the same noise in both directions), a fixed budget of N = 8,192 examples (so N/B steps), and a random start, w1 ~ N(0, 1) and w2 ~ N(0, 0.1). The argument has an expiry date. It needs αhmax to be small, and the limit α < 2/hmax does not move with the batch. Once the best step size gets near that limit, a larger batch still lowers the noise but can no longer buy a proportionally larger step, and the flat direction gets fewer steps to finish. Past that point a larger batch just wastes data. McCandlish and colleagues called the turning point the critical batch size; Zhang and colleagues showed the NQM reproduces it, and that it depends on the optimizer.

Fit the linear rule η* = c·B to the toy’s tuned SGD step sizes at B = 1 to 64. They fit it almost perfectly. Now use the rule at B = 512 (16 steps). What happens at the extrapolated step size?

  1. Within 10% of the best loss at B = 512
  2. About twice the best loss
  3. The run diverges
  4. Better than the tuned step: the rule extrapolates past what a grid finds

It diverges derived. The rule gives η ≈ 0.000589 × 512 ≈ 0.30, above the stability limit 2/hmax = 0.2. The tuned step at B = 512 is 0.174, and even then the best loss is 5.7 times the B = 1 value. A rule fitted where its assumption holds says nothing about where the assumption breaks, and nothing in the fit warns you. Problem 3.1 of the assignment makes you find this range yourself.

Adam scales differently

For adaptive methods the analogous small-step argument suggests η* ∝ √B instead of ∝ B (the square-root rule of Malladi and colleagues). Real language models follow neither rule cleanly, and published scaling rules for the optimal learning rate disagree with each other; the assignment opens with a figure collecting them, from the Step Law paper. Assignment 2 has you measure the exponent for Adam in the toy, then in d8 at batch sizes 8 to 256. Section 7 shows that for AdamW the cleanest thing to scale with batch is not the learning rate alone.

4. Momentum and preconditioning pay at large batch

Momentum. With s ← μs + g and θ ← θ − ηs, a run that takes small steps behaves like SGD with step size η/(1 − μ): the sum of agreeing gradients is just a larger step. Once the step size is tuned, that buys nothing. The difference shows up when the stability limit binds. Heavy-ball momentum stays stable up to ηh < 2(1 + μ), so its effective step η/(1 − μ) can reach 2(1 + μ)/((1 − μ)hmax): 38/hmax at μ = 0.9, against SGD’s 2/hmax derived. The flat direction gets large steps while the steep one oscillates but does not blow up. Small batches need small steps anyway, because the noise floor is high, so the extra headroom is useless there. Large batches have a low floor and can use it.

Preconditioning. Zhang and colleagues model it as stepping with H−p: each direction gets step ηh−p. At p = ½ the toy’s curvature spread shrinks from 10 to √10, so the steep direction stops holding the flat one back. Adam’s division by √v is a rough, noisy version of this, and in their experiments Adam and K-FAC raised the critical batch well above SGD’s.

The sweep view puts numbers on both derived. Tuned momentum (μ and η both tuned) beats tuned SGD by 1.02× at B = 1, 1.7× at B = 16 and 7.3× at B = 512. Preconditioning beats it by 1.46× at B = 1 and 7.7× at B = 512. In the toy preconditioning helps even at batch 1, which differs from the paper. The reason is that the assignment’s noise is the same in both directions, so the preconditioner also cuts the noise injected along the steep one; Zhang and colleagues assumed the noise covariance equals the curvature, and under that assumption the small-batch gain disappears. One more trap: with μ fixed at 0.9 instead of tuned, momentum is worse than SGD at B ≥ 128 in the toy. Sixteen to sixty-four steps are too few for a memory that spans about ten of them. “Momentum helps at large batch” assumes enough steps for it to act.

5. Where the quadratic stops

The NQM is the workhorse of this unit because it is exactly solvable and often points the right way. It is also wrong about several things that matter, and knowing where is part of using it:

  • Curvature is not fixed. In full-batch training the sharpness rises until it hovers around 2/η, the very limit the quadratic says must not be crossed, and the loss keeps falling anyway while oscillating (the “edge of stability”, Cohen and colleagues). A quadratic would have diverged.
  • Noise is not a fixed Gaussian. Real gradient noise depends on where the weights are, is heavy-tailed, and is correlated with the curvature in ways that change during training.
  • Adam is not a fixed preconditioner. Its √v follows the noise as much as the curvature, and at small batch it mostly measures noise.
  • The landscape has structure a bowl cannot hold. Loss that drops sharply during a learning-rate decay, curves that cross after the decay, valleys with a slowly descending floor: unit 3 covers these with the “river valley” picture.
  • Weight norms change the effective step. In a normalized network the same η means different things at different weight norms. The quadratic has no notion of this, and it is the subject of the rest of this unit.

The working rule: trust the NQM for the direction of an effect and for which regime you are in, and measure the size.

6. Why weights grow

CS 312’s first quiz contained a question that looks like bookkeeping. Four d8 runs cross the learning-rate schedule (linear decay or constant) with weight decay (0.1 or 0). Every step, each run logs the L2 norm of all its parameters and the L2 norm of its gradient before clipping. Predict before reading on.

At the end of training, which runs have a parameter norm noticeably (3–15%) below the highest value it reached during the run? The others end within 3% of their peak.

  1. All four: norms always come down as training settles
  2. Both runs with weight decay 0.1
  3. Only linear decay with weight decay 0.1
  4. None: every run ends at its peak

Only linear decay with weight decay 0.1 measured; the other three end within 3% of their peak. Without weight decay nothing pulls the norm down, so it ends at its maximum. With weight decay and a constant learning rate it reaches an equilibrium and stays there. Only when the learning rate falls does the equilibrium itself move down, and the norm follows it. The rest of this section and the next explain why.

Scale invariance

Most weight matrices in a transformer with RMSNorm feed, directly or a few operations later, into a normalization that divides out their scale. QK-norm normalizes queries and keys; the pre-norm before each block normalizes the residual stream. For such a weight, multiplying it by any c > 0 leaves the network’s output unchanged, or nearly: L(cw) = L(w). Two facts follow by differentiating that identity derived:

  • With respect to c at c = 1: w · ∇L(w) = 0. The gradient is orthogonal to the weight. Changing the weight’s length does nothing, so the gradient has no component along it.
  • With respect to w: ∇L(cw) = ∇L(w)/c. The gradient shrinks as the weight grows.

Now take a step u orthogonal to w. By Pythagoras, |w + u|² = |w|² + |u|²: every step makes the norm grow, whichever way it points. A larger norm means the same step turns the weight by a smaller angle, and the angle is all the loss cares about. For SGD the effect compounds, because the gradient itself shrinks like 1/|w|: the effective learning rate is η/|w|² (van Laarhoven, 2017). Without weight decay the effective learning rate decays on its own, on a schedule nobody chose and nobody logs. Weight decay multiplies the norm by 1 − ηλ every step, pulling it back. The question is where the two balance.

The equilibrium

Here is why the product is the knob. Take a scale-invariant weight w with d entries, trained with AdamW. Assume:

  1. each step u is orthogonal to w (scale invariance);
  2. its size is roughly constant, |u| ≈ ηρ√d, where ρ is the per-coordinate RMS of Adam’s update (Adam normalizes, so ρ does not track the gradient’s scale);
  3. successive steps point in roughly unrelated directions, so they add like a random walk, not coherently;
  4. ηλ ≪ 1, and the run is long compared with 1/(2ηλ) steps.

Each step shrinks w by 1 − ηλ and adds an orthogonal |u|:

|w′|² = (1 − ηλ)² |w|² + η²ρ²d
equilibrium:  |w|² = η²ρ²d / (1 − (1 − ηλ)²) ≈ ηρ²d / (2λ)
angle turned per step:  |u| / |w| ≈ ηρ√d / √(ηρ²d / 2λ) = √(2ηλ)

At equilibrium the angle each weight turns per step is about √(2ηλ) derived. It depends on η and λ only through their product, and not on ρ or d. Since the loss only sees the direction, the product is the effective learning rate; η alone sets only the equilibrium norm. Kosson and colleagues (2024) call this rotational equilibrium; the same algebra with SGD (gradient ∝ 1/|w|) gives the same √(2ηλ). The approach to equilibrium takes about 1/(2ηλ) steps. For d8’s defaults, 2ηλ = 6×10−4, so that is about 1,700 of its 9,375 steps, and the equilibrium angle is about 0.024 radians, 1.4° per step derived. Real updates are only approximately orthogonal and uncorrelated, so read these as orders of magnitude.

In the toy the step size is exactly constant, so the equilibrium is exact: |w|² = η/(λ(2 − ηλ)), and the angle per step is a function of ηλ alone derived. Things to try:

  • Set λ = 0. The path spirals outward forever and the angle per step falls like 1/√t. This is the run that ends at its norm peak in the quiz question, and why its effective learning rate is out of anyone’s control.
  • η = 0.01, λ = 0.1, then tick the twin (η = 0.04, λ = 0.025). The twin settles on a circle four times wider and turns by exactly the same angle per step. Their final losses match.
  • Switch the schedule to linear decay. With λ > 0 the norm peaks and then follows the shrinking equilibrium down, ending below its peak (17% at the defaults; d8 measured 3–15%). With λ = 0, or with a constant schedule, it ends at its peak. That is the first quiz answer above.
  • Try η = 0.001, λ = 0.01: the relaxation time is 50,000 steps, so the run never reaches equilibrium and the start still sets the effective step at the end.

Reading the measured results

The grid. If only ηλ mattered, the loss surface over (log η, log λ) would be a valley along lines of constant product. The measured grid is close to that: the cells that keep the best product (A and B) are the best of the rest, and penalties grow with how far the product is off. Not exactly, because η also matters on its own: it sets the step size of the parameters that are not scale-invariant (embeddings, gains, the readout), and the stability limit from section 2 still applies. That is why C, with η doubled as well, is worse than D.

The batch. At a fixed token budget, B = 512 means eight times fewer steps. If the right amount of rotation over the whole run is what matters, each step must rotate more, and the timescale 1/(ηλ) must shrink eight times when measured in steps. Wang and Aitchison read AdamW as an exponential moving average of updates with that timescale. Bergsma and colleagues (Power Lines) found that the best timescale, expressed as a fraction of the training data, B/(ηλD), stays roughly constant, which makes the right ηλ proportional to B. Recipes C, D and E all satisfy that. D wins because it gets the whole factor from λ, which has no stability limit, while C puts it all into η, which does. Quiz 2’s B = 256 setting in section 4 (η = 0.0015, λ = 1.944) is this rule applied.

Your move: the batch doubles in the middle of a run. Which single change keeps the rotation per token the same, and why not just raise η?

Double λ. After the switch there are half as many steps per token, so each step must rotate √2 times more: √(2ηλ) × √2 means ηλ × 2. Doubling η also doubles the product, but it doubles the step size of the parameters that are not scale-invariant too, and moves you toward the stability limit. Doubling λ changes only the equilibrium. Assignment 2 has a worked example question on exactly this switch, with five policies to rank; try it before reading their answer.

8. Hyperball: fix the norm, keep the angle

If the learning rate and weight decay matter mostly through the norm equilibrium they produce, why not set the norm directly? Hyperball (Wen and colleagues, 2026), used in assignment 2, does this for every linear-layer weight matrix. Take Adam’s update direction U, scale it relative to the weight, step, then project back to the weight’s initial Frobenius norm R = |W0|F:

W̃ = W − η |W|F U / |U|F      W ← R · W̃ / |W̃|F

No weight decay is needed, because the norm cannot drift. The relative step is η on every step, so the angle turned per step is about arctan η, whatever happened earlier in the run. The learning rate becomes a number with a meaning: the fraction of the weight’s norm to move per step. Switch the widget above to Hyperball to see it: the radius never changes and the angle per step stays fixed at arctan η.

Two subtleties the lecture flags. Not every weight should be norm-constrained: the readout’s scale encodes the model’s confidence, and embeddings may use their norms, so the course applies Hyperball to the internal matrices and the readout, while embeddings and normalization gains use Adam at 0.1041η. And the idea has relatives: weight normalization, steps on the sphere or on the Stiefel manifold, column-wise norms, ball projection. Wen and colleagues report lower loss and norm control by construction; the open question in the assignment is whether it also makes the best learning rate scale more predictably.

CS 312 fitted Hyperball’s optimal peak learning rate on d8 (weight decay off) at four token budgets. Read off their plot, the fitted optima are roughly 0.014, 0.013, 0.012 and 0.010 at 0.15B, 0.31B, 0.61B and 1.23B tokens measured (approximate, read from a figure). Which interval contains the fitted optimum at 2.46B tokens?

  1. 0.0075 or more
  2. 0.0055 to 0.0075
  3. 0.004 to 0.0055
  4. Less than 0.004

About 0.008 measured. The trend continued. A straight line in log–log through the four points gives about 0.009 derived; the last two alone give about 0.008. The answer slides call it an easy question for that reason: follow the line. Whether AdamW’s ladder in assignment problem 1 is as regular is exactly what the assignment asks you to find out.

9. Audit drills

Three fictional research logs, each written like an AI research agent’s notes. Each one breaks a rule from this unit while sounding reasonable. Tick every objection you would raise, then check. The director’s checklist from unit 0 applies throughout.

[agent] Project: chess-move model, 60M params. Baseline B=128, AdamW lr 2e-3, wd 0.1, cosine schedule (config: total_steps=37,500, warmup=750), 4.9B tokens, val 1.874. [agent] Throughput: B=256 is 1.7x faster per token on our GPUs. Switched batch to 256, everything else from the same config. 4.9B tokens = 18,750 steps. [agent] Result: val 1.906 (+0.032). Seed spread on this model is ~0.004 (3 seeds), so the gap is real. [agent] Conclusion: larger batches hurt this model, consistent with the generalization gap of large-batch training. Reverting to B=128 and dropping the batch-size line of work.

Which objections would you raise?

  • The schedule still targets 37,500 steps, but the run stopped at 18,750: it ended halfway through the cosine, with the learning rate still near its middle value.
    A config that counts in steps silently changed meaning when the batch doubled. The run was compared before its decay, and on most recipes the decay alone is worth more than 0.03.
  • Learning rate and weight decay were kept from B=128 although the run takes half as many steps; at least λ (or the product ηλ) needed to scale with the batch.
    With half the steps and the same ηλ, each step rotates the weights too little over the run. CS 312’s batch-512 result says keeping the old pair is a large loss and scaling λ is the cheap fix.
  • “Larger batches hurt” is a claim about every setting; the run tested one setting at B=256.
    The honest conclusion is “B=256 with B=128’s settings is worse”. Dropping the line of work on that basis throws away a 1.7× throughput gain on an untuned comparison.
  • A gap of 0.032 could be seed noise.
    Not the problem here: three seeds give a rough spread, but 0.032 is eight times 0.004. The gap is real; what caused it is the question.
  • The large-batch generalization gap explains the result.
    Not the problem, but a wrong explanation to accept: that story is about overfitting a fixed training set. In one-pass pretraining, train and validation loss move together; what large batches cost here is steps.
  • The agent should have used gradient accumulation instead of a real batch of 256.
    Not the problem: accumulation gives the same gradient as a larger batch (up to numerics). It changes memory, not the optimization.

Two of the checklist’s questions fail here: was each arm tuned (no), and were the runs compared after the decay (no, and nobody noticed because the config hid it). The repair: express the schedule in tokens, rerun B=256 at λ × 2 and at η × √2 (two runs), and compare after the decay. The self-review checked whether the gap was larger than the noise, which it was, and stopped there.

[agent] Project: small code model, 120M params, AdamW baseline tuned over lr {5e-4, 1e-3, 2e-3} x wd {0.05, 0.1, 0.2}: best lr 1e-3, wd 0.1, val 1.412. [agent] Trying Lion (sign of an interpolated momentum, betas 0.9/0.99). Same lr 1e-3, wd 0.1, schedule, data order. 1 seed. [agent] Lion: loss spikes at step 1,800 and 4,100, recovers; final val 1.561. [agent] Lion is 0.149 worse. Seed spread ~0.005, so ~30 sigma. [agent] Conclusion: Lion is not suited to code data. Closing the ticket.

Which objections would you raise?

  • Lion ran at AdamW’s tuned learning rate. Its sign update has magnitude 1 in every coordinate, far larger than Adam’s typical step, so the same η is a much larger effective learning rate.
    The loss spikes are the symptom. Lion’s authors recommend a learning rate 3–10 times smaller than AdamW’s. Comparing at one borrowed rate compares how well 1e-3 suits each optimizer.
  • Weight decay was also borrowed. With decoupled decay the product ηλ sets the equilibrium; lowering η for Lion without raising λ would change it again.
    The Lion authors scale weight decay up by the same factor they scale the learning rate down, which keeps ηλ fixed. A fair Lion arm needs a small grid, as AdamW got.
  • “Not suited to code data” names a cause the experiment did not vary: no other data, and no tuned Lion run.
    The only thing shown is that Lion at AdamW’s settings is worse on this data. A conclusion about the data needs the same comparison on other data, after tuning.
  • One seed is not enough to call a 0.149 gap.
    Not the problem here: with a seed spread around 0.005 the gap is not noise. More seeds will not fix an untuned comparison; they will confirm it.
  • Lion uses less optimizer memory, so it should have been compared at a larger batch.
    Not the problem: memory savings are a separate argument. Changing the batch for one arm adds a confound instead of removing one.

Checklist question 1 (was each arm tuned) fails in the most common way: the baseline got a 3 × 3 grid and the challenger got the baseline’s winner. Question 5 would have caught it too: one Lion run at lr 3e-4 would likely have changed the picture. The repair is a Lion grid around lr 1e-4 to 3e-4 with wd 0.3 to 1.0, the same size as AdamW’s.

[agent] Project: symbolic-music model (MIDI tokens), 45M params, AdamW lr 3e-3, wd 0.1 applied to ALL params including RMSNorm gains. Baseline val 1.208 (3 seeds: 1.205 / 1.208 / 1.211). [agent] Cleanup "bridge" run toward the new recipe: (1) exclude norm gains and biases from weight decay, (2) warmup 1% -> 3%. Seed 0. [agent] Result: val 1.248. Delta +0.040, within the noise of a single seed. Adopting as the new baseline so later changes compare against it.

Which objections would you raise?

  • 0.040 is not within the noise: the three baseline seeds span 0.006, so it is about 13 standard deviations.
    The agent called it noise without using the noise estimate sitting in its own log. Something in the bridge made the model worse.
  • Two changes in one run: if something is worse, there is no way to tell whether it came from the decay mask or the warmup.
    “What else changed” has two answers here. Each costs one run to separate.
  • Adopting a worse run as the baseline moves the reference point: every later change will be measured against a model that is 0.04 worse for an unknown reason.
    Later “wins” may just recover the 0.04. That is how a research log ends up proud of fixing its own regression.
  • Norm gains should never be excluded from weight decay.
    Not the problem as a rule: excluding gains and biases is the standard practice and the d8 default. But the change is not free; it may interact with the learning rate, which is one more reason to test it alone.
  • Warmup never matters by the end of training, so change (2) can be ignored.
    Not the problem, and false as stated: removing warmup entirely cost d8 about 0.10 in unit 1. Going from 1% to 3% is probably small, but “probably small” is what the separate run is for.

The worst move in this log is not the bundling. It is declaring the result noise without looking at the noise, then making the regression the new reference point. The repair: rerun the baseline from the same commit; run the two changes separately at seeds 0 and 1; adopt only a version that is no worse than the old baseline within its seed spread.

Check your understanding

In the noisy quadratic, what limits the step size and what limits how fast the run finishes?

The steepest direction limits the step size: α < 2/hmax, or it diverges. The flattest direction limits the duration: it needs about 1/(αhmin) steps. The gap between the two is why preconditioning, which narrows the curvature spread, helps.

Why does the best SGD learning rate grow linearly with batch size, and why does that stop?

For small steps, both the leftover from the start and the noise floor depend on α/B at a fixed number of examples, so doubling both leaves the outcome unchanged. It stops when α approaches 2/hmax: the steep direction caps the step, and beyond that larger batches only reduce the number of steps. That turning point is the critical batch.

Why does momentum help at large batch but not at small batch, according to the NQM?

At small steps momentum is SGD with step η/(1 − μ), which tuning matches. Small batches need small steps because of the noise floor. Large batches can use large steps, and momentum stays stable up to ηh < 2(1 + μ), so its effective step can exceed SGD’s 2/hmax. Measured on d8: removing momentum cost about 0.03 at B = 8 and 0.25 at B = 256.

Why do scale-invariant weights grow under gradient descent?

Scale invariance makes the gradient orthogonal to the weight, so every step adds its squared length to the squared norm (Pythagoras). Nothing removes it unless weight decay does.

At equilibrium under AdamW, what sets the angle a scale-invariant weight turns per step, and what does η alone set?

The angle is about √(2ηλ): the product. η alone sets the equilibrium norm, |w|² ≈ ηρ²d/(2λ), and the step size of the parameters that are not scale-invariant.

A batch goes from 64 to 512 at a fixed token budget. Which change did CS 312 measure as best, and what does keeping the old settings cost?

Multiplying weight decay by 8, which multiplies ηλ by 8 (the Power Lines rule ηλ ∝ B) without pushing the learning rate toward instability. Keeping the batch-64 pair cost about 0.106.

What does Hyperball change, and what does its learning rate mean?

It scales each matrix’s Adam step relative to the weight’s norm and projects back to the initial norm, with no weight decay. The norm cannot drift, so η is the relative step per update, about the angle turned per step.
